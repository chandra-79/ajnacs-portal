Hypothetical · Logistics planning

Evaluate arrival intervals before changing delivery commitments

Hypothetical ETA forecasting with interval coverage, width and operational review measured separately.

#Proposed forecasting problem

A hypothetical logistics team wants more informative arrival estimates for internal dispatch planning. The proposed model would estimate arrival time and an uncertainty interval for eligible shipments. It would not automatically change customer delivery promises or contractual commitments. All shipments, intervals and results in this case are synthetic.

Discovery would define the prediction checkpoint, actual-arrival event and treatment of cancelled or rerouted shipments. Carrier timestamps and time zones would be reconciled. The team would identify information available at the checkpoint, excluding later scans that reveal the eventual outcome. A forecast generated at departure should not be evaluated using features first observed near arrival.

#Architecture and delivery

A feature pipeline would combine approved route, schedule and tracking data with explicit availability times. The model output would carry issue time, horizon, version and data-quality status. A separate policy would determine how planners use the interval. Missing tracking updates would trigger a documented fallback rather than a narrow interval based on stale evidence.

Implementation would compare a route-based baseline with candidate forecasts on later periods and unseen routes where appropriate. Tests would include time-zone transitions, delayed scans, rerouting and a new carrier. TimeSeriesSplit provides background for time-aware evaluation, but route grouping and forecast horizons require additional design.

#Synthetic interval evaluation

Suppose a test contains 100 completed shipments. Configuration A's intervals contain the actual arrival for ninety shipments and have an average width of six hours. Configuration B covers ninety-five shipments with an average width of ten hours. The chart shows observed coverage counts of ninety and ninety-five; widths are reported separately because they use a different unit.

Coverage is 90/100 = 90% and 95/100 = 95%. The five-percentage-point gain comes with four additional hours of average interval width. These invented figures do not establish that B is better: a wider interval may be too vague for dispatch, while A may miss too many important arrivals. A finite test's observed coverage is not a guarantee for future shipments.

#Acceptance and operation

Acceptance would consider coverage, width, point-error measures and performance by route, horizon and data freshness. The team would inspect the ten or five uncovered cases rather than hiding them behind an average. A limited pilot would measure whether planners make better scheduling decisions and whether the interface communicates uncertainty clearly.

Rollback would restore the baseline estimate and retain historical issued forecasts for later comparison. Handover would include timestamp semantics, interval methodology, eligible-route definitions and a rehearsal of a carrier-feed outage. The principal limitation is structural change: disruption, new routes or altered carrier behavior can invalidate historical patterns, so the operating design must make stale evidence and unsupported populations visible.
