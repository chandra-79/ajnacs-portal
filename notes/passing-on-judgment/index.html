Pass on the judgment, not just the answer

What an experienced person can make visible so that the next person learns how to choose, not only what was chosen.

Chandra Lanka 24 Nov 2026 5 min read

On this page

  1. Tell the story before the outcome became obvious
  2. Name the cues that mattered
  3. Compare two reasonable choices
  4. Include the recovery from a mistaken judgment
  5. Build a small judgment exercise

An experienced person can often recognise a problem before they can explain every step that led to the recognition. A familiar pattern appears, a detail feels wrong, or an option seems unsuitable. The answer arrives with the speed of practice.

That speed can be useful and difficult to teach. A learner sees the conclusion but not the distinctions that produced it. “You will know with experience” may be true in part, but it leaves the learner without a way to practise the relevant kind of attention.

Passing on judgment means making some of those distinctions visible. It does not require pretending that every decision follows a clean formula. It requires showing what you noticed, what alternatives you considered, what would have changed your mind, and where the current answer stops applying.

#Tell the story before the outcome became obvious

A completed incident or project can make its earlier uncertainty disappear. Once the cause is known, the useful signal stands out and the distracting evidence looks unimportant. A learner who receives only that cleaned-up story may get a misleading picture of how the work actually felt at the time.

Reconstruct the decision from the information available then. What did the first observation suggest? Which explanations were plausible? What made one check more useful than another? Which result caused the investigation to change direction?

A hypothetical performance problem might initially look like insufficient compute capacity. A closer look could show that requests spend most of their time waiting for a constrained dependency. The teaching value lies partly in explaining why adding workers was considered and which observation made that response less promising.

Preserve uncertainty without manufacturing drama. There is no need to exaggerate confusion or create a heroic discovery. A plain account of the branching reasoning is often more useful than a story built around the brilliance of the final answer.

#Name the cues that mattered

Experts sometimes describe a choice as obvious because several small cues combine quickly in their mind. Slow down enough to identify those cues. Was it the timing of the failure, the boundary between two components, the mismatch between a metric and a user report, or the absence of an expected signal?

Then distinguish a cue from a rule. A particular symptom may suggest a class of causes without proving one. A learner needs to know whether the cue should trigger a hypothesis, an immediate action, or a required safety check.

Give a counterexample where the same cue would mean something different. A rising queue can indicate insufficient processing capacity, but it can also reflect a changed arrival pattern or a downstream constraint. The important habit is to inspect the conditions rather than memorise a single remedy.

This is where teaching can preserve humility in expertise. The experienced person is not handing over an infallible pattern library. They are showing how patterns become useful when checked against the current situation.

#Compare two reasonable choices

A lesson that presents one sensible option against one absurd option teaches little about judgment. Real decisions often involve alternatives that each protect something valuable and impose a cost elsewhere.

For a fictional release, one choice may preserve a deadline by reducing scope. Another may retain the scope and delay delivery. A third may stage the release so that a limited group can use the completed portion. The discussion should identify whose needs each choice serves and what evidence would make it acceptable.

Ask the learner to make a provisional choice before hearing the experienced person's conclusion. Then compare the reasoning rather than merely the answer. Two people may choose the same option for different reasons, and only one set of reasons may survive a changed condition.

Michael Nygard’s architecture decision-record essay offers a way to preserve alternatives and context. A teaching conversation can use a lighter version: choice, reasons, trade-offs, and a condition that would trigger review.

#Include the recovery from a mistaken judgment

Experience contains mistaken calls as well as successful ones. A useful account explains how the mistake was detected, what consequence it had, and how the process changed afterward. This makes correction part of competence rather than an exception to it.

Avoid asking learners to imitate a mistake's consequences in an unsafe environment. Use a bounded simulation, a historical public example, or a clearly fictional scenario. The lesson is in recognising and responding to the error, not in recreating unnecessary harm.

Also avoid turning the story into a confession that the learner must comfort. Keep the focus on the work: the assumption, the missing signal, the correction, and the revised boundary. A calm account makes it easier for someone else to reveal their own uncertainty later.

Google's discussion of blameless postmortems emphasises learning from the conditions surrounding an incident. A teaching conversation can preserve that same interest in how the work happened without reducing the explanation to a person's carelessness.

#Build a small judgment exercise

Choose a decision that occurs repeatedly in your work. Write a short scenario with enough context for a learner to propose a next step. Include one detail that matters and one that is tempting but inconclusive. Do not make the exercise a guessing game about what the teacher prefers.

Ask the learner to name their first action, the evidence they expect, and what would make them change direction. After discussing the answer, alter one condition and ask again. The changed condition reveals whether the reasoning is tied to the mechanism or only to the surface story.

End by naming what the exercise did not cover. A learner who handles one scenario well has practised a useful distinction; they have not acquired unlimited authority in the domain. Give them a clear route for further practice and for asking when a real situation exceeds the exercise.

Read the steps
  1. Expose the early evidence. Reconstruct what was known before the outcome.
