Make the decision without pretending the map is complete

Good judgment can name uncertainty, choose a proportionate action, and preserve a way to learn what happens next.

Chandra Lanka 24 Nov 2026 4 min read

On this page

  1. Mark the parts of the map differently
  2. Match the action to the cost of being wrong
  3. Leave a decision record that can survive new information

A decision rarely arrives with every relevant fact arranged neatly around it. Some information is unavailable, some is expensive to obtain, and some concerns a future that cannot be observed in advance. Waiting for a complete map may be impossible. Acting as though the map is complete creates a different problem: uncertainty disappears from the explanation while remaining in the world.

A more useful approach is to make the incompleteness explicit and decide what to do within it. This involves several distinct questions. What is known? Which assumptions matter? What would be costly to reverse? What information could arrive soon enough to change the choice? The answers do not remove uncertainty, but they can make action proportionate to it.

This is a practical form of judgment rather than a display of confidence. A person can speak decisively about the next step while remaining honest about the limits of the reasoning. The action may be narrow, temporary, or designed to gather evidence. Its value comes partly from matching what is known rather than demanding a grand conclusion from incomplete material.

#Mark the parts of the map differently

Consider a hypothetical team deciding whether to change an onboarding process. It knows that several users asked for help at one stage. It assumes that the instructions are the main cause. It does not yet know whether the problem affects most users or a particular group. These statements should not be blended into a single claim that the process is broken because the documentation is poor.

Write observations, interpretations, and open questions separately. An observation is what was actually seen or recorded. An interpretation explains what it might mean. An open question identifies information that could alter that explanation. The categories are not a philosophical exercise. They show where a small investigation might change the decision and where additional discussion would merely repeat what is already known.

Some assumptions are more important than others. A minor estimate may have little effect on the choice. An assumption about whether a change is reversible may determine the whole plan. Focus attention on assumptions that could lead to a different action if they were false. This helps avoid spending time refining details while leaving the decisive uncertainty unexamined.

It can be useful to ask someone who was not involved to read the map. They may notice a conclusion presented as a fact or a missing dependency that everyone inside the discussion has taken for granted. Give them a specific task: identify the assumption most likely to change the decision. A bounded review can be more useful than an invitation to challenge everything.

#Match the action to the cost of being wrong

A reversible experiment allows a different posture from a commitment that changes many people's work or removes an important option. The distinction is not perfectly binary. Reversal may be technically possible but socially expensive, or it may restore the configuration without undoing its consequences. Describe what reversing would actually require rather than simply labeling the decision reversible.

For the hypothetical onboarding problem, a small revision tested with a few willing readers may reveal useful information at modest cost. Replacing the entire process across all teams creates a broader commitment. The smaller action is not automatically superior; it may fail to test an important interaction. The point is to choose an action whose scale fits the evidence needed and the consequences involved.

A staged decision can preserve options. Agree on an initial step, the evidence to inspect, and the point at which a larger commitment will be considered. This is different from repeatedly postponing a choice without learning anything. Each stage should have a purpose and a condition for moving forward, changing direction, or stopping.

There are situations in which delay is itself costly. A team may need to act before a preferred investigation can finish. In that case, name the uncertainty and select a response that protects the most important obligations as far as the available evidence allows. Record what must be checked afterward. Urgency changes the available method; it does not make assumptions into facts.

#Leave a decision record that can survive new information

A useful record includes the question, the available evidence, the important assumptions, the chosen action, and the reason it was proportionate. It should also name what would trigger reconsideration. This is more helpful than a retrospective story that makes the decision appear inevitable once its outcome is known.

The record protects learning in both directions. A good outcome does not prove that every assumption was correct. A bad outcome does not prove that the decision was unreasonable given the information available. Reviewing the original reasoning allows the team to distinguish process quality, new information, and chance without rewriting history to fit the result.

For a reflection exercise, choose a decision that is currently stalled. Write the smallest action that would either meet the immediate need or resolve an important uncertainty. Then identify its possible downside and what reversal would involve. If the action is too broad, narrow it. If it cannot provide useful evidence, choose a different question to investigate.

Set a review trigger that can be observed. It might be a date, a completed trial, a change in workload, or the arrival of a specific piece of information. Avoid a vague promise to revisit when more is known. Someone should know what they are waiting for and who will bring the decision back into view.

The note on saying no considers how boundaries shape commitments. Incomplete information adds another boundary: what the current evidence can responsibly support. Respecting that boundary does not require paralysis. It may lead to a smaller commitment, a clearer experiment, or a decision whose conditions remain visible.

A map can be incomplete and still useful if its blank areas are marked. The same is true of a decision. The aim is not to eliminate uncertainty through confident language. It is to act with enough clarity about evidence, consequence, and revision that the next piece of information has somewhere to go.
