A handover is a conversation with someone asleep

Working across time zones means designing the exchange, not asking everyone to stretch the same day.

Chandra Lanka 24 Nov 2026 4 min read

On this page

  1. Write for the next working session
  2. Make the request answerable in one turn
  3. Protect overlap for the difficult work
  4. Close the loop explicitly
  5. Try the sleeping-colleague test

A message sent at the end of one person's day can become the beginning of another person's uncertainty. The sender has supplied the answer they had time to write. The recipient discovers that one missing detail prevents the next step. By the time the clarification returns, a working day may have passed.

This is not necessarily a problem of effort or responsiveness. It may be a problem in the shape of the handover. When people share little working time, a vague request costs more because the next conversational turn is further away.

The answer is not to make everyone permanently available. It is to make more exchanges complete enough to survive a period of silence. That requires clearer context, explicit decisions, and a considerate account of what the other person can do without waiting.

#Write for the next working session

A useful handover says what happened, what matters now, and what action is needed. It includes the relevant record, the current state, and any constraint that would make an apparently reasonable action unsafe.

Consider a fictional investigation passed between two teams. “Still failing; can you check?” leaves the recipient to reconstruct the work. A better handover identifies the failing request, the environment, the checks already performed, the result of each, and the next hypothesis worth testing. It also says which changes have not been made.

The handover should distinguish facts from guesses. A log entry is an observation. A suspected cause is a hypothesis. Label both so the next person can continue the investigation without inheriting uncertainty as certainty.

Avoid copying an entire conversation when a short synthesis would serve better. A transcript preserves everything and explains little. Link the underlying record, then provide the minimum current account needed to begin safely. The recipient can follow the detail when it becomes relevant.

#Make the request answerable in one turn

Before sending a question, imagine the obvious clarification the other person will need. Supply it if you can. If you are asking for a review, identify the section, the decision it supports, and when an answer is useful. If you are asking for a choice, present the alternatives and the constraints that matter.

This does not mean predicting every possible question. It means removing avoidable ambiguity. A recipient should not have to ask which version of the document you mean or whether a deadline is in their time zone.

Use explicit dates and time zones for consequential timing. Relative phrases such as tomorrow or end of day are easy to misread across a distributed group. When no exact time is needed, say which working session the answer should support rather than manufacturing urgency.

Provide a default only when the person receiving it has the authority to accept that default. “If no objection arrives, we will change production access” is not a considerate shortcut when approval is required. Silence must not be converted into consent merely because conversation is inconvenient.

#Protect overlap for the difficult work

A small period of shared time can be valuable. Use it for ambiguity, disagreement, sensitive feedback, or a decision that benefits from rapid questions. Avoid spending all of it reading aloud the status that could have been written beforehand.

Prepare a short set of unresolved points. For each, state what is blocking progress and who can resolve it. If a point is only informational, move it into the written update. This leaves more of the overlap for work that genuinely needs a conversation.

The cost of inconvenient meeting times should also be visible. If one group always attends outside its usual hours, the arrangement is not neutral merely because everyone appears on the same call. Review whether the timing can rotate or whether the meeting can be replaced with a different exchange.

People's circumstances differ, so avoid assuming that an early morning is easier than a late evening for everyone. Ask about workable windows and respect the answer. Coordination improves when constraints can be stated without requiring personal disclosure.

#Close the loop explicitly

A handover is incomplete when the sender cannot tell whether it was received, understood, or acted upon. A short acknowledgment can identify the next action and reveal a misunderstanding before it becomes a full day's work.

The receiver might say that the current hypothesis is understood, the proposed check will run in a safe environment, and the result will be added to the shared record. If a prerequisite is missing, name it immediately rather than waiting until the end of the session.

When the work is complete, update the record that the next person will consult. Do not leave the resolution only in a private message. Distributed work becomes much harder when each time zone holds a different part of the truth.

A closed loop does not require instant replies. It requires a shared expectation about acknowledgment and completion. Teams can define that expectation around their real overlap and support obligations instead of assuming that every message deserves immediate attention.

#Try the sleeping-colleague test

Take a request you plan to send near the end of your day. Assume the recipient cannot ask you a follow-up question before beginning. Read the request from that position. Can they identify the correct artifact, the desired action, the authority they have, and the conditions under which they should stop?

Add the missing pieces, then remove detail that does not affect the next action. A handover should be complete enough to use and short enough to enter. Link deeper history instead of placing all of it in the opening paragraph.

After the next exchange, ask which clarification was still needed. Use that feedback to improve the handover pattern rather than blaming either person's location.
