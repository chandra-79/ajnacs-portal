Practice something that does not need to be published

Private practice gives a skill room to be awkward before it has to become a useful result.

Chandra Lanka 24 Nov 2026 4 min read

On this page

  1. Choose a difficulty small enough to see
  2. Let the attempt leave evidence
  3. A practice session you can finish

A piece of work changes character when it has an audience. The audience may be a customer, a colleague, a public timeline, or simply an imagined future reader. Its presence can sharpen the work. It can also make every attempt feel as though it needs to justify itself as a result. There is less room for an exercise that ends with a better question and nothing worth displaying.

Private practice makes a different agreement. The work exists to expose a difficulty, test a technique, or become more familiar with a material. It does not have to prove competence to anyone. A small program can be discarded. A paragraph can be rewritten several ways. A diagram can reveal that the idea was unclear without becoming evidence that its author should never draw diagrams.

This is not a claim that privacy automatically makes practice effective. Repeating the same comfortable task can remain comfortable forever. A useful exercise still needs a purpose and a way to notice what happened. The difference is that its result is allowed to be learning rather than a polished object. That permission is easy to lose when every activity is expected to become content.

#Choose a difficulty small enough to see

Suppose, in a hypothetical example, someone wants to become better at explaining a technical decision. Writing an entire guide may be too large a practice task. It contains research, structure, examples, editing, and uncertainty about the audience. Choosing one decision and explaining it in a single page makes the difficulty easier to inspect. The exercise can ask whether a reader would understand the tradeoff without needing the meeting that preceded it.

The same principle applies to technical work. Someone exploring an unfamiliar API might build one disposable request path, deliberately send an invalid input, and inspect the response. The point is not to create a miniature product with branding and deployment. It is to make one behavior concrete. A small boundary keeps the exercise from turning into another unfinished project.

Write down what you are practicing before beginning. Clear naming is different from concise naming. Explaining a mechanism is different from persuading someone to adopt it. Reading an unfamiliar error is different from memorizing a command. If the aim remains vague, it is easy to judge the exercise by whatever happens to go well and avoid the part that was meant to be difficult.

Set an ending condition too. You might stop when you can explain one distinction, reproduce one behavior, or identify the next uncertainty. This is not a contest to finish quickly. It is a way to keep practice from expanding until it competes with every other responsibility. A small completed exercise is easier to return to than a large obligation disguised as curiosity.

#Let the attempt leave evidence

A private exercise can still produce a useful record. Keep the first explanation beside the revision. Save the input that caused confusion. Note the assumption that turned out to be wrong. These traces make it possible to see what changed without needing a public score. They also make a future attempt easier to begin because the question has not disappeared into memory.

Avoid grading every exercise as a success or failure. A more informative review asks what became clearer, what remained uncertain, and which part of the task was harder than expected. Sometimes the useful discovery is that the exercise was poorly chosen. That is still information. It may suggest that a prerequisite needs attention or that the real problem sits elsewhere.

There is value in occasionally asking another person to inspect the work, but choose the question carefully. Asking whether the result is good invites a broad judgment. Asking where an explanation becomes difficult to follow invites specific help. The feedback should serve the practice objective rather than convert the exercise into an audition for approval.

Public sharing can come later if the work becomes useful to others. It need not be forbidden, and privacy need not become a way to avoid all criticism. The useful distinction is between work designed to learn and work designed to communicate a result. They can be connected without being identical. An unfinished experiment does not need to be presented as advice just because it produced an interesting screenshot.

#A practice session you can finish

Choose one small skill that appears in your ordinary work. Write a sentence naming the skill and a sentence naming the evidence you will inspect. If the skill is explaining a diagram, the evidence might be whether each arrow has a clear meaning. If it is reading a query plan, the evidence might be whether you can distinguish an estimate from an observed result in one example.

Prepare a modest exercise using non-sensitive material. Complete an attempt, inspect it against the stated question, and make one revision. Then write three short notes: what you tried, what changed, and what you would try next. Stop there unless continuing is a deliberate choice. The exercise should leave a doorway for another session, not a demand that another session must happen immediately.

Notice the temptation to make the record more impressive than the work. Adding a polished presentation, a complex toolchain, or an ambitious title can move attention away from the skill being practiced. Sometimes those additions are relevant. Often they are another task. Keep them only if they help answer the original question.

There is also no need to practice every weakness at once. A professional life contains many skills, and some are more useful to develop now than others. Choose a difficulty connected to work you care about, not merely one that makes you feel behind. Private practice should offer room to grow, not another inventory of personal inadequacies.

The note on accumulating knowledge considers how learning changes as experience grows. Private practice gives that learning a manageable place. It lets a capable person remain a beginner at something specific, work through the awkwardness, and keep the result in proportion. Some useful work deserves an audience. Some useful work simply makes the next attempt better.

Read the steps
  1. Name one difficulty. Choose a small skill and an observable question.
