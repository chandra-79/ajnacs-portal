Share the question before it becomes a proposal

An unresolved question can invite useful collaboration when its context and boundaries are clear.

Chandra Lanka 24 Nov 2026 4 min read

On this page

  1. Offer the material, not a disguised conclusion
  2. Protect the exploratory status
  3. A note short enough to send for review

People often wait to share an idea until it resembles a proposal. The reasoning has been organized, the awkward parts have been hidden or repaired, and a recommendation gives the conversation a clear destination. That can be useful when a decision is needed. It can be less useful when the most important work is still discovering what the question should be.

An unfinished question offers a different kind of invitation. It says that there is a problem worth examining and that the person raising it does not yet have a complete answer. This can create room for other people's experience before the shape of the solution becomes difficult to change. The invitation works best when it is specific enough that others know how to help.

Simply announcing confusion is not always enough. A colleague receiving a vague concern may have to reconstruct the whole context before contributing. Sharing early should not mean transferring all the work of framing to someone else. A useful unfinished question includes the observed problem, why it matters, what has been considered, and the part that remains uncertain.

#Offer the material, not a disguised conclusion

Imagine a hypothetical team wondering why new users struggle with an onboarding step. One person suspects that the instructions are too long. If they arrive with a proposal to shorten the instructions, discussion may focus on editing. If they arrive with examples of where users stop, the group may discover a missing permission, an unclear prerequisite, or a confusing choice. The initial question leaves more explanations available.

That openness should be real. A proposal disguised as a question can be frustrating: what do you think of this approach, when the decision has already been made and alternatives are unwelcome? If there is a fixed constraint or a preferred direction, state it. People can work within limits more effectively when the limits are visible.

Likewise, explain what kind of contribution would help. You might need a counterexample, a relevant source, experience with a similar situation, or a check on your assumptions. These are different requests. Naming one gives a busy colleague a manageable way to participate without agreeing to solve the entire problem.

Use a small amount of evidence. One concrete example can be more useful than a long history of unease. Distinguish the example from the general claim you are considering. If three people reported difficulty, say that three reports prompted the question; do not turn them into a universal explanation of user behavior. Early discussion benefits from evidence whose limits remain visible.

#Protect the exploratory status

An unfinished question can become an accidental commitment when it moves through an organization. Someone hears that a team is considering a change and reports that the change is planned. A sketch becomes a roadmap item. A possible date becomes a promise. Label the status clearly enough that the material can travel without gaining certainty it has not earned.

This does not require elaborate warnings. A short statement that the note is exploratory, that no implementation decision has been made, and that the current request is for evidence can be sufficient. Put the statement near the question, where a reader will see it before interpreting the details. Status is part of the meaning of the artifact.

The conversation also needs an owner. Sharing a question does not remove responsibility for following it through. Someone should collect the useful responses, identify the next investigation, and decide when the group has enough information to make a proposal or set the question aside. Otherwise early collaboration can become a place where interesting problems accumulate without resolution.

There should be room to discover that no project is needed. Perhaps the reported difficulty has a small local fix, the evidence is too weak to justify a larger intervention, or another team is already addressing it. An exploratory question has done useful work if it prevents unnecessary work. It does not need to become a funded initiative to count as a good conversation.

#A note short enough to send for review

Try writing an unfinished-question note with five elements: the observation, the consequence, the current explanations, the uncertainty, and the specific help requested. Keep each element brief. The note should let a reader decide whether they have relevant knowledge before asking them to absorb a large amount of context.

For example, a hypothetical documentation question might state that several readers reached the same step and asked for clarification, that this delays completion, and that the writer is unsure whether the missing piece is terminology or a prerequisite. The request could be for one colleague to read that step using only the linked material. That is a bounded investigation, not a request for general thoughts about the entire documentation system.

Before sharing, remove language that makes your preferred explanation sound like an established fact. Replace obvious with the evidence you actually have. Replace users always with the observed cases. This does not make the note timid. It gives other people room to contribute information that could change the interpretation rather than merely approve the framing.

After the discussion, publish a short update in the same place. State what became clearer, what remains open, and what happens next. Credit contributions where appropriate. The update closes the loop for people who gave attention and prevents an old exploratory note from remaining the only visible account of the question.

The note on feedback concerns how people help improve work already taking shape. Sharing an unfinished question moves that help earlier, when the framing itself can still change. It asks for a different form of generosity: curiosity about a problem that has not yet become a persuasive story.

There is room in professional work for both well-formed proposals and clearly framed uncertainty. The proposal helps a group decide. The unfinished question helps it avoid deciding too soon what the problem must be. Used with care, it gives collaboration a chance to affect the work before collaboration becomes another word for reviewing an answer someone has already chosen.

Read the steps
  1. Show the observation. Describe the concrete event that prompted the question.
  2. Name the uncertainty. Separate possible explanations from established facts.
  3. Request specific help. Ask for evidence, a counterexample, or a bounded review.
  4. Close the loop. Record what changed and who owns the next step.
