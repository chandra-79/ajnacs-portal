Leave the work where someone can find it

Finishing a task includes making its result understandable, discoverable, and safe for the next person to use.

Chandra Lanka 18 Jan 2026 5 min read

On this page

  1. Name things for the reader's search
  2. Put the answer near the entrance
  3. Make status visible
  4. Connect the record to the place people already visit
  5. End one task with a handover to an absent reader

A useful piece of work can effectively disappear without being deleted. It sits in an unnamed document, a private message, an old branch, or a folder whose title made sense during one busy afternoon. The person who needs it knows it exists but cannot tell which version is current or whether it is safe to use.

Findability is easy to treat as housekeeping after the real work. It is also part of whether the work can help anyone. A decision that cannot be found must be reconstructed. A result without its method must be trusted on reputation. A guide without an owner can remain persuasive after it becomes wrong.

Leaving work findable is an ordinary form of consideration. It asks what the next person will know when they arrive and what they should not have to guess. That next person may be a colleague, a newcomer, or the original author after enough time has passed.

#Name things for the reader's search

A document title should help a person distinguish it from nearby documents. “Notes” and “Final” describe the author's activity, not the reader's need. “Checkout recovery test: assumptions and results” gives a future reader a subject and a purpose.

Dates can help when time is part of the identity, such as a particular exercise or meeting. They are less useful as a substitute for a stable home for an evolving procedure. A folder full of dated “latest” runbooks leaves the reader to infer which one carries authority.

Use a stable entry point for current instructions and preserve historical records separately when they matter. The current page can link to the decision or experiment that explains its origin. History remains available without competing with the instructions a person needs now.

This is not an argument for elaborate naming conventions everywhere. A naming scheme that requires a handbook to decode can become another barrier. Prefer familiar words, a clear subject, and the minimum structure needed to distinguish purpose, status, and scope.

#Put the answer near the entrance

A reader should not have to retrace the author's entire investigation to learn what was decided. Start with the result or current instruction. Then explain the reasoning, alternatives, evidence, and limitations that make it assessable.

A hypothetical restore-test note might begin by stating whether the tested recovery objective was met, which dataset and environment were used, and what remains untested. The detailed timeline can follow. That order lets a reader decide whether the record is relevant before spending time on the full account.

Avoid hiding a decisive limitation at the end. If the test restored only a small sample, that belongs beside the result. If the guide requires a particular permission or software version, put it before the command that depends on it. Context is most useful immediately before it changes interpretation or action.

This also helps people reading under pressure. Someone recovering a service may need the next safe step before they have time to understand the entire architecture. A calm explanation can remain available below the operational instruction.

#Make status visible

Draft, proposed, accepted, superseded, and archived are different states. A polished page does not tell a reader which state it occupies. Neither does the fact that it was shared by a senior person. Status should be explicit when misunderstanding it would change behaviour.

An accepted decision can also have a review condition. Perhaps it applies until a contract changes or until a test reveals an unmet requirement. Recording that condition gives the future reader a way to recognise when the document deserves attention.

Ownership matters for the same reason. The owner need not be the original author forever. It should be a person or team able to answer whether the material still applies. If no one owns an old instruction, say so and avoid presenting it as a maintained operational route.

For architectural choices, decision records provide a useful structure. For smaller work, a brief status line and a link to the current owner may be enough. Match the ceremony to the consequences of getting the status wrong.

#Connect the record to the place people already visit

A beautifully organised folder is not enough if nobody knows to look there. Put the record near the workflow it supports. Link a release decision from the release checklist. Link an onboarding guide from the access request. Link an experiment's method from the chart that reports its outcome.

A useful test is to start from the reader's problem rather than the author's file location. If someone asks how to recover a failed deployment, where would they naturally begin? Can that entry point lead to the maintained procedure without private knowledge?

Links should have descriptive text. “Click here” makes a page harder to scan and gives little information when encountered out of context. A link called “Recovery test method and limitations” tells the reader why they might follow it.

Check the reverse relationship too. A procedure may depend on a decision that has since changed. Linking those records helps a reviewer discover the affected material when the decision is revised. Findability supports maintenance as well as initial discovery.

#End one task with a handover to an absent reader

Choose a recently completed item and imagine that the next reader cannot ask you a question. Give them a title, a short statement of purpose, the current result or instruction, and the most important limitation. Add the source material needed to check the work.

Then identify the entry point from which they should find it. Place one useful link there. Do not create a new catalog unless the existing places genuinely cannot serve the need. A small connection that people use is better than a comprehensive index nobody visits.

Ask a colleague to locate the item from that entry point and explain what they believe they are allowed to do with it. Notice any uncertainty about status or scope. Revise those points before adding more detail.

The final act of a task can be a short act of hospitality: making the result easier for someone else to enter. That is different from documenting every keystroke. It is preserving the part of the work that another person will need to understand, trust, continue, or responsibly change.
