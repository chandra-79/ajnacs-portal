Changing your mind without rewriting the past

How to revise a position clearly while preserving the reasoning that made the earlier choice understandable.

Chandra Lanka 18 Apr 2026 5 min read

On this page

  1. Keep the old reasoning visible
  2. Name the size of the revision
  3. Give the correction a destination
  4. Make disagreement survivable before you need it
  5. Write the update before the next meeting

Changing a position is easy to describe as a virtue and awkward to do in a room full of witnesses. Yesterday's confident recommendation becomes today's exception. The person who raised the concern remembers the exchange. The document remembers it too.

One response is to pretend the new position was always the intended one. Another is to offer an elaborate apology that makes everybody manage the speaker's discomfort. A more useful response is smaller: say what you believed, what changed, and what you now recommend. Then help the people affected adjust their work.

Professional credibility need not depend on appearing unchanged. It can depend on making changes in judgment understandable. A person whose reasoning can be inspected is easier to collaborate with than a person whose certainty must be protected.

#Keep the old reasoning visible

Suppose, in a fictional planning discussion, a team chooses a managed service because it expects a modest workload and has limited operational capacity. A later integration reveals a constraint that makes the service unsuitable. It would be misleading to describe the original decision as obviously careless without considering what was known at the time.

It would be equally misleading to defend the decision forever because it was reasonable once. A good explanation distinguishes the quality of the earlier process from the suitability of the current choice. The original assumptions can remain intelligible while the recommendation changes.

This is one reason a short decision record can be helpful. The record should preserve the context, important alternatives, assumptions, and expected consequences. A later note can explain which assumption failed. You do not need to erase the old document or stage a public trial of its author.

Michael Nygard’s essay on architecture decision records discusses the technical practice. The personal habit is broader: write down enough of your reasoning that a future correction does not require reconstructing your own motives from memory.

#Name the size of the revision

Not every new fact requires abandoning a position. Sometimes it changes a boundary: the approach still works for internal tools but not for a public service. Sometimes it changes the order of work: the migration remains useful, but the identity model must be settled first. Sometimes it overturns the recommendation entirely.

Being precise about that size prevents confusion. “We need to rethink everything” can sound decisive while leaving people unable to tell which work remains valid. “The latency assumption was wrong for remote users; keep the data model, pause the rollout, and test regional access” gives the revision an operational shape.

There is also value in naming what did not change when it is directly relevant to the decision. This is not an invitation to surround every correction with defensive qualifications. It is a way to protect useful work from unnecessary churn. People should be able to distinguish a revised conclusion from a complete restart.

A change of mind should include its confidence. You may have enough evidence to stop a risky rollout without yet knowing the best replacement. Say both. Otherwise an honest rejection of one option can be mistaken for certainty about another.

#Give the correction a destination

A correction announced in a conversation may never reach the people acting on the earlier advice. The practical question is where that advice travelled. Was it copied into a proposal, a training guide, a backlog, or a purchasing request? Update the places that still carry authority.

This is the unglamorous part of intellectual humility. It is easy to say that new evidence changed your view. It takes more care to revise the instructions, notify the affected owner, and remove the outdated example from a document somebody will read next month.

The update should be proportionate. A minor wording improvement does not need a company-wide announcement. A changed release condition needs to reach the people preparing the release. A published technical claim needs a visible correction or revision note where readers can encounter it.

Do not make the person who raised the concern do all this work. Acknowledge their contribution, especially if it was initially dismissed, then carry your share of the follow-through. Otherwise the organisation learns that finding a mistake earns extra administration while confidently making one does not.

#Make disagreement survivable before you need it

It is difficult to invite correction after rewarding only agreement. If a team watches questions get treated as disloyalty, a later request for honest feedback will not erase that history. People will reasonably wait to see what happens when someone disagrees on a matter that costs the leader something.

A useful habit is to state one condition that would change a recommendation when first making it. For example: if a representative recovery test cannot meet the agreed objective, the design must return for review. This creates a place for evidence to enter without requiring an argument about character.

NASA's decision-analysis guidance treats assumptions and uncertainty as part of comparing alternatives. A small team need not reproduce a large organisation's process. It can borrow the discipline of making the decision's conditions explicit.

This does not mean every opinion needs a formal reversal threshold. Casual preferences can remain casual. Reserve the effort for judgments that commit other people's time, expose them to risk, or become instructions they are expected to follow.

#Write the update before the next meeting

Choose a recent recommendation that no longer fits the available evidence. Write four sentences: the earlier recommendation, the assumption it depended on, the new information, and the revised action. Avoid “obviously,” “as everyone knows,” and other phrases that make the past sound easier than it was.

Add one practical detail: who needs the update and where the original recommendation remains visible. If you are only reconsidering, say that rather than manufacturing a finished conclusion. Ask a colleague to read the note and tell you what they would now do differently.

The aim is not to perform humility. It is to make the work safer to revise. A team can move with conviction and still leave room for a fact that changes the route. The useful reputation is not being the person who never moves. It is being the person whose movement makes sense.
