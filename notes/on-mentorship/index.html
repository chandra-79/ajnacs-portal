On mentorship — what I got wrong for years

Chandra Lanka 4 Aug 2025 Updated 23 Nov 2026 3 min read

I was a bad mentor for a long time without knowing it.

Not malicious — inattentive. I gave advice generously, answered questions thoroughly, connected people to opportunities when I could. By the visible metrics of mentorship, I was doing the right things. What I was actually doing was treating every conversation as a consulting engagement: identify the problem, provide the solution, move on.

That's not mentorship. That's advice-giving from a position of experience.

I understood the difference when I had a mentor who worked differently.

What she did differently: she asked questions I didn't know the answers to. Not difficult technical questions — questions about what I actually wanted, what I was avoiding, what I was afraid of. She was less interested in helping me solve the problem I presented with than in helping me see the problem underneath it.

The result was that the conversations changed me rather than just informing me. That's the distinction.

The version of mentorship I was practising was efficient but shallow. I could generate useful advice at speed because I'd encountered similar situations before. The result was that people left conversations with better answers to the questions they'd asked. They rarely left with different questions.

The other thing I got wrong: I mentored in my own image. When someone came to me with a career question, I implicitly evaluated their options against the path I'd taken and the things I valued. This is a hard bias to see from inside it. Someone who values security differently than I do, or who has family constraints I don't have, or who is optimizing for something I've never prioritized — they don't need my framework. They need their own framework, examined clearly.

The version of mentorship I'm trying to practise now: fewer answers, more questions. Being genuinely curious about what the person wants, not what I would want in their position. Sitting with "I don't know" rather than filling the silence with analogies from my own experience.

I'm still not good enough at it. But I know now what I'm trying to do.

#Let the question get a little less convenient

Imagine someone asking whether to take on a lead role. The tempting response is to compare the salary, the title, and the learning opportunity. That comparison is useful after you know what the person is trying to decide. The same question could be about whether they still enjoy the work, whether they can manage another team's dependencies, or whether accepting would leave them with less time at home. None of those is settled by demonstrating that the job is a promotion. Asking what they would be sorry to give up may change the conversation more than describing the advantages of the role.

A generous question gives the person a chance to surprise you. A question designed to lead them to the decision you already favour is advice with more steps. You can offer your perspective plainly if they want it. It helps to describe the conditions under which your own example made sense and let them decide whether those conditions apply. There is no requirement that they end up admiring the same things about the job that you do.

#Leave a decision in the right hands

The work can become more concrete before the conversation ends. Suppose the person worries that the role will be mostly meetings and cannot tell from the description. They could ask the hiring manager to walk through an actual week and identify a recent decision the lead owned. They might then speak to someone already doing similar work. Those are ways to learn about the opportunity before deciding. They leave the person with room to do something other than accept or decline immediately.

It can also help to agree on what should come back to the next conversation. Give them time to compare the picture they had with what they found. Ask which part of the decision feels clearer and which constraint still needs attention. If their preference changes, you have something useful to discuss. If it stays the same, it may now rest on information they can explain. Both count as progress. The decision is theirs, and that includes the right to make a choice that would make less sense for the mentor.
