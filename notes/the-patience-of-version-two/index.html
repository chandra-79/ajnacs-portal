Give the second version a different job

Revision is more useful when it tests the purpose of a piece of work instead of merely polishing its surface.

Chandra Lanka 24 Nov 2026 5 min read

On this page

  1. Read for purpose before reading for polish
  2. Make three passes with different questions
  3. Keep enough distance to notice what the draft assumes

The first version of an explanation often does a job that the reader never sees: it helps the writer discover what they think. Sentences arrive in the order the ideas become available. Examples remain because they were useful during exploration. A conclusion appears near the end because that is where the writer finally found it. This can be a valuable draft and a difficult finished piece.

The second version has a different responsibility. It must help someone else understand the result without repeating the writer's entire route to it. That requires more than improving individual sentences. The order, emphasis, and amount of context may need to change. A paragraph that was essential to discovering the idea may be unnecessary for explaining it.

This distinction can make revision less personal. Removing a passage does not mean the time spent writing it was wasted. The passage may have completed its first job. Keeping it merely to prove that work occurred asks the reader to pay for a history they do not need. The useful question is what the next version should make possible.

#Read for purpose before reading for polish

Consider a hypothetical proposal intended to help a team choose between two approaches. Its first draft contains extensive background, several interesting alternatives, and a recommendation in the final paragraph. A surface edit can make every sentence clearer while leaving the reader uncertain about the decision. The second version should first ask whether the proposal makes the choice, constraints, and consequences visible.

Write the intended reader's question above the draft. It might be why this change is needed, how a mechanism works, or what evidence supports a claim. Then inspect each section against that question. Some material answers it directly. Some supplies necessary context. Some is interesting but belongs elsewhere. These categories are more useful than asking whether each paragraph is good in isolation.

This is also the moment to check the promise made by the title and introduction. A title that promises a practical guide creates different expectations from one that offers a personal reflection. If the draft contains a thoughtful question rather than a settled method, adjust the promise. Revision can improve honesty as well as clarity by making the frame match the evidence.

Do not rush to delete everything that is not immediately functional. An example, a pause, or a small observation can help the reader stay oriented. The test is whether it contributes to understanding or merely preserves the writer's attachment. There is room for texture in clear writing. There is less room for material whose only justification is that it took effort to produce.

#Make three passes with different questions

A useful revision can separate structure, support, and language. In the structure pass, ask what the reader needs first and how each part prepares the next. In the support pass, ask which claims need evidence, examples, or qualification. In the language pass, ask where the wording makes the reader work unnecessarily. These passes can be brief; their value is keeping different problems distinguishable.

The order matters in a practical sense. Spending an hour polishing a paragraph that later moves into a separate article may be enjoyable, but it may not serve the current task. Likewise, simplifying a sentence cannot repair an unsupported claim. Work on the largest uncertainty that affects the piece before refining details that depend on it.

A revision should also inspect its examples. Are they representative of the point being made? Do they carry hidden assumptions? Does a hypothetical scenario look like a report of something that actually happened? Clear labels protect the reader from mistaking illustration for evidence. An example can explain a mechanism without claiming that its numbers describe the world.

If someone else reviews the draft, give them the question for the current pass. Asking for any thoughts can produce comments at every level, from argument to punctuation. That may be useful sometimes, but it can also scatter attention. Asking whether the decision is understandable or whether a particular transition is supported makes the review easier to use.

#Keep enough distance to notice what the draft assumes

A writer knows the missing connections because they were present while composing. A reader does not. One way to inspect this gap is to summarize each paragraph in a short phrase and read only the phrases. The outline may reveal a leap, a repeated point, or a long detour. Another is to read the draft as though you disagree with its recommendation and look for the question it fails to answer.

Neither technique is a ritual that every piece requires. Choose the one that addresses the difficulty you actually see. A short note may need only a clearer opening. A consequential technical guide may require checking every example against its source. The effort should match the purpose and the cost of misunderstanding, not a fixed number of editing rounds.

For a small exercise, take a draft that feels almost finished. Before changing any sentence, write three lines: what the reader should understand, what they should be able to do, and what the piece does not establish. Then make one structural change that brings the draft closer to those lines. Only afterward choose a paragraph to polish.

Save a brief note explaining the change. Perhaps the recommendation moved earlier, an unsupported claim became a question, or a large example became a separate link. This record can help you recognize useful revision patterns without turning editing into a score. The aim is to become more deliberate about what a second version is for.

There is a stopping point. Revision can become a way to postpone allowing the work to be used. Decide what adequate means for the current purpose, check the important requirements, and release the piece when they are met. A later version can respond to actual questions from readers rather than an endless imagined audience.

The note on reading well offers the other side of this exchange. A reader brings limited attention and a real question. The second version respects both by making the writer's discovered understanding easier to enter. It does not erase the first draft's labor. It turns that labor into something another person can use.

Read the steps
  1. Name the reader’s question. State what this version should help someone understand.
  2. Rebuild the order. Place the necessary ideas in a usable sequence.
  3. Check the support. Inspect claims, examples, and limits before polishing.
  4. Finish for the purpose. Meet the current requirements and leave room for later work.
