Notice what became normal

A familiar workaround can be either sensible adaptation or a clue that the work has quietly accepted an avoidable burden.

Chandra Lanka 22 Nov 2026 4 min read

On this page

  1. Begin with description, not accusation
  2. Watch for work that disappears from the official account
  3. Separate inconvenience from a signal
  4. Change the smallest consequential part
  5. Take a familiar task for a walk

A newcomer asks why a task requires three approvals, two spreadsheets, and a message to a person whose name is not in the procedure. The answer arrives quickly: that is how it works here. Familiarity has made the sequence feel like part of the landscape.

Some local practices have good reasons that are not immediately visible. A control may protect a real boundary. A seemingly redundant check may catch a failure that once caused serious trouble. The newcomer may need context before judging the process.

Other practices persist because everyone has learned to work around them. The original reason has disappeared, but the effort remains. Noticing what became normal means being willing to inspect familiar friction without assuming that every tradition is either wisdom or waste.

#Begin with description, not accusation

A question about a routine can sound like criticism of the people who maintain it. Start by describing the sequence and asking what each step protects or enables. This gives people a chance to explain the function before defending the history.

In a fictional access process, a manual approval may exist because the automated path cannot distinguish two classes of data. Removing the approval without addressing that distinction would weaken a control. The useful improvement might be a clearer classification or a narrower automated route, rather than simply deleting the human step.

A different approval may be a relic of an ownership structure that no longer exists. If no current responsibility depends on it, the step may be a candidate for retirement. The same surface inconvenience can have a different meaning in different contexts.

Ask for the current reason. “We have always done it” explains persistence, not necessity. A historical incident may supply valuable context, but the response should still be checked against the present system and its present risks.

#Watch for work that disappears from the official account

A process may appear simple because the difficult parts happen outside its documented boundary. An onboarding form is submitted, and later access appears. Between those events, an experienced colleague may chase several owners, correct missing fields, and interpret ambiguous responses.

That hidden work matters. It consumes capacity and can make the process unreliable when the experienced colleague is unavailable. A measurement that begins after their intervention may describe a smooth path that newcomers cannot actually enter alone.

Ask people to narrate a recent ordinary case, including the messages and checks they do almost automatically. Do not treat this as surveillance or a search for someone to blame. The goal is to understand what the system currently requires to function.

A useful record distinguishes hands-on effort from elapsed waiting. Both may matter, but they suggest different interventions. A task that takes five minutes of action and several days of waiting is not improved in the same way as one that consumes hours of repeated manual handling.

#Separate inconvenience from a signal

Not every annoyance deserves a redesign. A small awkwardness may be acceptable compared with the cost of changing a mature process. The task is to identify which friction affects a meaningful outcome: reliability, accessibility, timeliness, clarity, or the ability to operate without private knowledge.

Look for recurrence and consequence. Does the same confusion affect many newcomers? Does an unclear handover repeatedly delay a decision? Does a workaround bypass a control or depend on information unavailable to the people expected to perform it?

A single example can reveal a mechanism worth investigating, but it does not establish prevalence. Check a bounded sample or compare several recent cases before claiming that the whole process is broken. Preserve exceptions that show where the routine works well.

The platform onboarding guide offers one setting for this kind of inquiry. Completion time becomes more informative when the start, finish, eligible attempts, and uncompleted cases are all defined rather than selected for a favourable story.

#Change the smallest consequential part

Once a burden is understood, the improvement need not be a replacement of everything around it. A clearer prerequisite, a named owner, a better error message, or a reliable status update may remove much of the confusion.

Choose a change that can be evaluated against the identified problem. If the problem is that people cannot tell whether a request is waiting or lost, adding a status and escalation route may be more useful than redesigning the entire form.

Keep the protection supplied by the old step unless the need for it has been explicitly reassessed. An improvement that makes the happy path shorter while making errors harder to detect may simply move the burden elsewhere.

Invite the people who perform the work to test the revision. They may notice an edge case that is invisible in the process diagram. Their adaptation is evidence about the current system and a resource for designing the next version.

#Take a familiar task for a walk

Choose one routine you can perform almost without thinking. Write the visible steps, then add the private knowledge you use between them. Ask which parts a new colleague could discover from the maintained instructions.

For each awkward step, write its current purpose or mark the purpose unknown. Investigate one unknown with the responsible owner. If the purpose remains valid, improve its explanation or execution. If it no longer applies, discuss a safe retirement.

Do not turn the exercise into a campaign against every local convention. The aim is selective attention. A team needs stability as well as change, and an established practice should be understood before it is replaced.

Read the steps
  1. Describe the routine. Include the private work between visible steps.

  3. Locate the important burden. Check recurrence and effects before redesigning.
