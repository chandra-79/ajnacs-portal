@source US Department of Education · Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning | https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1

## Write a question you can score

Select one useful mechanism from a reading session. Draft ten short questions whose answers can be verified, such as why a timeout can raise retries, what units identify the bottleneck, and what outcome would contradict that explanation. Close the source and answer the questions without looking. Score against the same rubric after a delay. Explain the reasoning in your own words and compare it with the article only after recording the answer.

## Measure transfer separately

The example gets nine of ten right immediately and six of ten after a delay. A later transfer task scores five of ten using a comparable rubric on a new example. Immediate recall, delayed recall, and applying an idea to unfamiliar work measure different things. The chart can prompt a reading plan. It should not be interpreted as losses in a single identical construct.

Use errors to choose the next exercise. If a learner recalls a formula but cannot apply it, give a new example with a different unit or boundary. Preserve successful strategies and re-test an equivalent question without displaying the answer during the attempt.

## Limits of the example

These are example scores for a single learner. Practice effects and unequal question difficulty can distort the difference. Keep the record for learning decisions. Avoid turning it into an employee performance rating.

@source US Department of Education · Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning | https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1

## Make criteria observable

An architecture exercise can assess whether the learner states the outcome, distinguishes assumptions from observations, identifies a reversible step, and explains how to check the result. Record examples that meet and miss each criterion. Invite a second reviewer to score the same artifact independently before discussing it. Focus feedback on a concrete example and a next attempt.

## Use disagreement to improve the rubric

Suppose reviewers agree on six of ten judgments. After clarifying terminology with worked examples, eight of ten judgments agree on a comparable exercise. Treat those rates as checks of the rubric, not as evidence that participants learned more. Percent agreement also does not adjust for expected agreement by chance. Inspect disagreements rather than relying on the aggregate alone.

Write down one change to the rubric before the next exercise. For example, replace “rollback is described” with “the learner defines the last reversible step and a check that the previous version is healthy.” Ask the learner to retry a new problem, keeping comparable time and instructions. Record what changed in the work itself.

## Limits of the example

The counts are illustrative. A small, familiar review group can agree because it shares the same assumptions. Check clarity with different reviewers and preserve disputed cases. Do not infer causality from score changes across unequal tasks.

# Test whether a team practice helps without overclaiming
@lesson team-practice-experiment | Compare a small engineering practice change against a baseline and keep participation, rework, and workload mix visible.
@chart Review completed within two days | %
@value Baseline | 12/20
@value Trial | 15/20
@match focus | meeting | work life | boundaries | interruption | career | learning | practice
@source NIST · Engineering Statistics Handbook guidance on comparisons | https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/

## Pick one outcome

Choose a change you can reverse, such as adding a protected review block. Define the sample before starting. In this example, a review counts as complete within two business days if the author has a usable decision or clear next step by the deadline. Record opened reviews, work type, eligible reviewers, and the first usable decision. Include reviews that never received one.

## Read the effect with context

In the example, 12 of 20 reviews meet the deadline before the trial and 15 of 20 during it. That is a gain of 15 percentage points. It could reflect the practice, smaller changes, simpler work, or who happened to be available. Inspect the same work-size categories and track rework. A higher completion rate that produces more repeat reviews may simply move time downstream.

Try an alternating schedule or compare equivalent teams if possible. Declare a short test window, define when a practice is stopped, and check that it did not transfer work onto another team member. Preserve the minimum data needed to make the decision and discuss the result with participants.

## Limits of the example

The chart uses illustrative outcomes and a small sample. A local trial can support a modest operational adjustment. It cannot establish a general productivity claim or justify monitoring individual behavior.
