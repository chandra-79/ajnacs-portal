Capacity planning was supposed to be one of the disciplines the cloud made obsolete — why forecast when you can autoscale? A decade of production experience delivered the correction: elasticity moved capacity planning from *procurement* (buy servers eighteen months ahead) to *engineering* (ensure the system can actually absorb demand growth), and teams that dropped the discipline entirely rediscovered it through its failure modes — the quota that capped scale-out during the biggest launch of the year, the database that autoscaling couldn't help, the cloud bill that doubled because "elastic" also means "elastic upward, silently." Capacity planning in the cloud era is the practice of knowing your **constraints, headroom, and demand trajectory** well enough that neither incidents nor invoices arrive as surprises.

## Demand: model the driver, not the metric

Infrastructure metrics (CPU, RPS) are *consequences*; planning starts from **business drivers** — users, orders, tenants, data volume — and the translation function between them ("each active tenant adds ~N queries/sec and ~M GB/year"). This two-layer model is what lets engineering consume the business's forecasts (marketing knows the campaign calendar; sales knows the enterprise-deal pipeline whose single onboarding might add 20% to load) and what makes capacity conversations legible upward — "we can absorb 3× current tenants before the primary database becomes the constraint" is a sentence executives can act on; "CPU is at 60%" is not.

Model three demand components separately, because they plan differently: **organic growth** (trend-fit from telemetry — with seasonality honestly included; retail's November is not July times a constant), **step events** (launches, campaigns, migrations, big-customer onboardings — calendar-driven, planned with their owners), and **burst behavior** (the daily peak-to-trough ratio and the worst-case spike shape — which sizes the *elasticity requirement*, distinct from the volume requirement). The classic planning failure is modeling only the first.

## Supply: find the real constraints before they find you

Every system has a **binding constraint** — the resource that hits its wall first — and it's rarely the one on the default dashboard. The constraint archaeology worth doing per critical service: the obvious scalables (compute fleets — usually fine, that's what autoscaling does), the **stateful chokepoints** (the primary database's write capacity, connection limits, and vertical ceiling — the classic true constraint, because scale-*up* has a top SKU and scale-*out* of writes is an architecture project, not a knob), the **quota layer** (cloud API rate limits, instance-type availability per zone, IP space in subnets, load-balancer and NAT gateway throughput ceilings — the invisible walls that have capped more launches than hardware ever did; inventory them, monitor consumption against them, and file the increases *before* the event), and the **external dependencies** (the payment processor's TPS contract, the partner API's rate limit, the license tier — capacity constraints wearing business-relationship costumes).

For each constraint, the two numbers that constitute actual knowledge: **current utilization at peak** and **validated maximum** — where validated means load-tested, not spec-sheet-believed (the capacity-test discipline from load-testing practice; extrapolated maximums bend under real workloads). Headroom is the gap between them, and its erosion rate — this quarter's peak versus last quarter's against the same ceiling — is the single most valuable chart in the whole practice: it converts "are we okay?" into "we cross the line in about seven months, and the fix has a four-month lead time."

## The trade: utilization is not the goal, survivable headroom is

FinOps pressure pushes utilization up; reliability pushes it down; capacity planning is where the trade gets made *explicitly* instead of by whoever shouted last. The framework: required headroom per service = **failure absorption** (lose a zone: the survivors must carry the load — N+1 or better for the critical tier, which alone implies meaningful idle capacity in steady state) + **burst absorption** (the spike shape faster than scale-out reacts — autoscaler lag, node boot, cache warm: the minutes during which *provisioned* capacity is all you have) + **growth buffer** (headroom consumed between planning cycles). A stateless tier with fast autoscaling might justify running hot (60–70% at peak); the primary database, whose "scale-up" is a maintenance window and whose failure absorbs nothing, earns its conservative 40%. Publishing the *target and reasoning per tier* ends the generic "our utilization is too low" conversation permanently — some of that idle capacity is engineered survival margin, priced and chosen; the rest is waste, and now you can tell them apart.

Commitment economics layer onto the same numbers: the demand model's *floor* (the load you're confident of for 1–3 years) is what reserved/committed pricing should cover; the variable crest rides on-demand and spot. Buying commitments without a demand model is how estates end up committed to yesterday's architecture — the FinOps failure mode with a capacity-planning root cause.

## The loop: quarterly rhythm, event-driven exceptions

The practice that works is neither the annual planning theater nor pure reactivity, but a light quarterly loop per critical system: refresh the demand model against actuals (was the forecast wrong? why?), re-measure constraint utilization and update the headroom-erosion chart, review upcoming step events with their business owners, and produce the one-page output — *constraints, time-to-wall at current trajectory, actions with owners and lead times*. Actions divide into buying capacity (quota increases, tier upgrades — cheap, fast, bounded) and **building capacity** (the read-replica strategy, the partition scheme, the cache layer — the architecture projects whose *lead time is the entire reason planning exists*: discovering in month eleven that you need the six-month sharding project is the failure the quarterly chart prevents). Event-driven exceptions (the surprise enterprise deal, the viral moment) get the same analysis compressed: which constraint does this hit, what's the fastest absorbing action, what breaks if we're wrong.

And close the loop with honesty metrics: forecast error (systematically optimistic models are common and correctable), incidents-with-capacity-root-cause (the discipline's failure counter), and headroom-purchased-but-never-approached (the over-provisioning counter — this practice cuts both ways). The end state worth aiming for is unglamorous and quietly powerful: capacity stops being an emergency genre entirely — growth arrives onto systems with engineered room, launches get absorbed by quota increases filed weeks prior, and the annual budget conversation starts from a demand model both finance and engineering already believe. In an elastic world, running out of capacity is almost always a planning failure wearing an infrastructure costume — and planning, unlike hardware, has no procurement lead time. It just has to be done.
