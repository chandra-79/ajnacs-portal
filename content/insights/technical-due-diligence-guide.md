Technical due diligence is the strange discipline of assessing years of engineering decisions in days, through a keyhole, while everyone you interview has rehearsed. The acquirer wants to know what the technology is *actually* worth — what it costs to run, extend, and integrate; what risks hide in it; whether the team that built it can keep building. The target wants the deal to close. Between those incentives sits the assessor, and the craft is knowing which observations are load-bearing — because in two weeks you cannot audit a system, but you can reliably read the *signatures* of engineering health, which predict the future better than any architecture diagram.

## What you're actually assessing (it's not the code)

The rookie framing is "is the code good?" — and it's the least useful question, both because code quality varies wildly within every codebase and because code is the most fixable layer. The senior framing assesses four things that are expensive to change:

**Architecture honesty** — not whether the architecture is fashionable, but whether the *description matches the reality* and the reality matches the business. A well-understood monolith with clean boundaries is a better asset than a "microservices platform" that's actually a distributed monolith requiring synchronized deploys (the Conway signatures are readable in the deploy calendar). The diagnostic gold is the *delta* between the slideware and what engineers describe under gentle specific questioning ("walk me through what happens when a customer places an order — which systems, which teams, what breaks").

**Delivery capability** — the DORA-shaped vitals, read from evidence rather than assertion: deployment frequency and lead time (visible in CI history and release logs — ask for the raw data, not the summary), change-failure signatures (the incident list, the hotfix ratio), test-and-pipeline reality (watch a deploy happen; the difference between "we have CI" and a trustworthy pipeline is an afternoon of observation). A company that ships weekly with small diffs can fix anything else; a company with quarterly release trains and manual regression cycles has told you the integration timeline regardless of what the code looks like.

**Dependency and liability surface** — the layer where deals acquire surprises: license compliance (copyleft in proprietary products remains a genuine deal-term item — SCA scans are cheap, run them), the security posture signatures (secrets handling, patch cadence against the CVE stream, the IAM sprawl indicators, whether there's ever been a pen test and what happened after it), data-protection reality versus policy (where personal data actually lives — including the logs, per the logging-hygiene truths — and whether deletion is implementable, because regulatory exposure transfers with the deal), and the vendor/infrastructure commitments (the pricing cliffs, the expiring enterprise agreements, the single-region architecture whose DR story is a diagram).

**Key-person and knowledge risk** — the assessment that most often changes deal terms: which systems have exactly one comprehender (the bus-factor mapping — ask "who would you call if X broke at 3 AM" across interviews and watch the same two names recur), what's written down versus tribal (runbooks, ADRs, onboarding time for recent hires as the honest documentation metric), and the retention reality of the people the deal's thesis quietly depends on.

## The craft: how to see through the keyhole

The access is limited and the presentations are polished, so the techniques matter: **triangulate artifacts against interviews** (the repo history, ticket system, and incident log are unrehearsed — commit patterns show the real team's shape, ticket age-distribution shows the real backlog, incident recurrence shows what postmortems actually change); **interview down the stack** (the CTO gives the vision; the senior engineer who's been there four years gives the archaeology — ask them what they'd fix first with a free quarter, then ask why it hasn't happened; the answer is usually the organizational finding); **watch for the honesty signature** — teams that volunteer their debt, name their fragile systems, and describe past incidents in mechanism-level detail are demonstrating exactly the engineering culture that makes debt fixable, while defensive polish on every answer is itself a finding; **run the cheap scans** (SCA, secrets scanning, dependency-age profiling — hours of tooling that surface objective conversation-starters); and **size the remediation, not the sin** — every finding gets an order-of-magnitude cost and a timeline ("the single-region posture is a two-quarter, N-engineer project"), because diligence output is an *investment model input*, not a code review.

## The output: findings that price, block, or plan

Structure the report by what the deal team can do with it: **value-adjusting findings** (run-cost realities, the re-platform the roadmap silently requires, the license remediation — numbers into the model), **term-adjusting findings** (key-person retention packages, escrow for compliance remediation, closing conditions on specific fixes), **integration-planning findings** (the identity-system merge, the data-model reconciliation, the delivery-cadence mismatch between acquirer and target — the items that dominate the first eighteen months and are cheaper to plan pre-close), and the rare **thesis-breaking findings** (the "AI platform" that's an outsourced demo, the architecture that cannot reach the scale the deal math assumes — the assessor's job on these is early, evidenced escalation, not report-padding). Everything gets confidence levels; two-week keyhole assessments that express certainty are advertising their own methodology gap.

And the mirror version is worth every technology leader's attention *before* any deal: the estate that would pass diligence — documented architecture matching reality, delivery metrics on dashboards, dependency hygiene automated, bus factors managed, incident learning visible — is simply a well-run estate; the diligence checklist is a health checklist with a deadline attached. Companies that run themselves as if the assessors arrive next quarter don't just close deals faster — they've internalized the discipline the assessment was ever trying to measure: engineering whose stated reality and actual reality are the same thing. That congruence, in the end, is what the two weeks are spent looking for — because everything else can be fixed, and its absence is the only finding that can't be remediated with budget.
