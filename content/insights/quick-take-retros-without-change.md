The retrospective runs. The team fills the board with post-its. The facilitator groups them. The team discusses. The hour ends. The board is photographed. The action items column is empty, or contains items that were never committed to with names and dates.

The next sprint is identical to the last one.

This is not a retrospective process problem. It is a retrospective outcome problem.

## What retrospectives are supposed to produce

A retrospective is an improvement meeting. The output is a specific set of changes to how the team works — changes that have a named owner, a target sprint or date, and enough specificity to know whether they were completed.

If the output is themes, observations, and discussion notes with no committed changes, the retrospective produced a record of problems, not a resolution of them.

## Why retrospectives produce themes instead of changes

**Facilitators conflate discussion with action.** A productive discussion about why the sprint was challenging is useful for shared understanding. It is not a substitute for a committed action. The facilitator who treats a rich discussion as a successful retrospective is optimizing for engagement over outcome.

**"More of" and "less of" items are not actionable.** "More cross-team communication." "Less interruption during focus time." "More documentation." These are desires, not actions. An action is: "Schedule a 30-minute weekly sync between our team and the data team, starting next sprint. [Name] owns the calendar invite." A desire has no owner and no completion criterion.

**Teams avoid committing to changes because changes require time.** A committed action item takes time to complete. That time comes from somewhere. Teams that are already overloaded resist committing to more work, even work that would make them less overloaded. The facilitator needs to help the team explicitly protect time for improvement work.

**Velocity pressure kills follow-through.** Action items from retrospectives compete with feature work for sprint capacity. Feature work has clearer business value and more visible stakeholder pressure. Retrospective actions get cut. The patterns that were identified persist.

## The format that produces outcomes

End every retrospective with a specific section: "What are we committing to change next sprint?" One to three items maximum. Each item has: what, who, when. The facilitator reads these aloud to confirm the team understands the commitments. The next retrospective opens with: what happened to the commitments from last time?

This makes commitments visible. A team should also record useful learning and explicit decisions not to add work when no intervention is justified.

## Begin with the previous decision

Open the next retrospective by reviewing the last experiment's evidence. Did the team actually try it? Was the result observable? Did the underlying problem change? A missed action can indicate insufficient capacity, an unclear owner or a plan that depended on somebody outside the team. Treat that information as part of the process being inspected rather than a reason to repeat the commitment more forcefully.

A retrospective does not require a new ticket every time. It can confirm that an existing change should continue, reveal that an assumption was wrong or decide that no intervention is currently worthwhile. Record that decision and its reasoning. The aim is adaptation where useful, not an action-item quota.

## Limit work in progress on improvement

Imagine a fictional team starting with ten open improvement ideas. It can fund four hours of improvement work in the next iteration. Two candidate experiments each need two hours; the other eight remain explicitly uncommitted. The chart compares ten ideas with two funded experiments. It does not imply that the eight deferred ideas lack value, only that the current plan has a capacity limit.

If the team instead labels all ten as commitments, the list conceals the same constraint. When most remain unfinished, participants may conclude that retrospectives never work. The more useful response is to distinguish ideas, selected experiments and verified changes. An idea becomes a commitment only when the needed time, authority and dependency are available.

## Facilitate a decision rather than a vote on personalities

Ask participants for a specific event and its effect on the work. Replace “the other team is unresponsive” with the request, the expected response and the actual wait. Invite alternative explanations. The receiving team may have lacked required information or may be operating under a different priority agreement. If they are not present, record a question to investigate rather than a conclusion about their motives.

For the selected experiment, write a hypothesis that can be disproved. For example, publishing a reviewer rota may reduce waits caused by unclear ownership. Decide what observation would count against that explanation. If waits remain concentrated around unusually large changes, the next intervention may need to address batch size instead. This turns disagreement into a way to learn rather than a contest over which story is most persuasive.

## Keep the review lightweight and visible

Use a record with the observation, hypothesis, experiment, coordinator, capacity allocation and review date. Attach only the evidence needed for the decision. A screenshot of a crowded board is not a substitute for knowing what the team selected and why. Equally, an elaborate improvement database can become another task that nobody has time to maintain.

At review, choose continue, adapt, stop or adopt as normal practice. For an adopted change, update the working agreement or procedure so new colleagues can discover it. For a stopped experiment, keep the reason. A team that can stop an ineffective change has learned something useful. The value of the retrospective lies in this decision cycle, not in whether every meeting ends with the same number of promises.
