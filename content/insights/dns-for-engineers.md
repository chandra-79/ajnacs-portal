"It's always DNS" earned its place as the industry's favorite eye-roll because it encodes a real asymmetry: DNS sits under *everything* — every service call, every database connection, every webhook and health check — while being the layer engineers examine last and understand shallowest. The result is a system whose failures masquerade as everyone else's (mysterious timeouts, regional weirdness, deploys that "randomly" break connectivity) until someone finally runs `dig` and finds the answer that was always going to be there. The corrective is modest: one honest mental model, a handful of failure patterns, and some operational hygiene.

## The model: a distributed, cached, eventually-consistent database

Resolution is delegation: the resolver walks from root servers to TLD servers to your zone's **authoritative** servers, which hold the actual records. But almost no query walks that path, because every layer **caches** aggressively — the recursive resolver (your VPC's, your ISP's, a public one), the operating system, the language runtime, sometimes the application's connection pool. DNS is therefore an *eventually-consistent* system whose convergence is governed by **TTLs** — and whose behavior in practice is governed by how much of the path actually honors them.

The TTL truths that explain most confusion: **caches serve until expiry, so a record change propagates as a decaying wave, not an event** — after you flip a record with a 300s TTL, clients converge over minutes *at best*; **some layers lie** — resolvers that clamp or extend TTLs, OSes and JVMs with their own cache policies (the JVM's historical cache-forever default has broken more failovers than any provider outage; know your runtime's `networkaddress.cache.ttl` story), and applications holding connections that never re-resolve at all (the pooled connection to a decommissioned IP is a failover classic: DNS updated perfectly, nothing reconnected — connection-lifetime policies and re-resolution on failure are the application-layer half of every DNS-based failover design); and **the pre-change ritual is mandatory** — drop the TTL *one full old-TTL period before* the migration, verify convergence, make the change, then restore. Teams that skip the ritual perform the migration twice.

## DNS as infrastructure layer: routing, failover, discovery

Modern DNS providers turned the phone book into a control plane, and using it well means knowing what each capability actually promises:

**Health-checked failover and routing policies** (weighted, latency-based, geo) make DNS the coarse-grained traffic-steering layer — the right tool for region-level failover and gradual traffic migration, with its honest caveat baked in from the section above: DNS steering converges at cache speed, so it's the *minutes* layer; anycast and load-balancer layers handle the *seconds* (the division of labor from multi-region practice). Design both, and don't ask DNS for what its physics can't give.

**Service discovery inside platforms** — Kubernetes runs on DNS (every Service a name, resolved constantly), which makes cluster DNS (CoreDNS) a tier-zero dependency with its own capacity and failure story: ndots-driven query amplification (the classic — every unqualified lookup fanning into multiple search-domain attempts, multiplying cluster DNS load), conntrack races dropping UDP lookups under load, and the mitigations (node-local DNS caching as near-default, sensible ndots, FQDNs in hot paths) that every Kubernetes estate eventually deploys — usually after the incident.

**CNAME/alias mechanics at the apex,** split-horizon zones (internal names invisible externally — and the audit question of what your internal zone *leaks* about your architecture when it isn't), and the provider-alias records that bend the CNAME-at-apex rule — the small standards knowledge that prevents the small standing mistakes.

## The failure patterns behind "it's always DNS"

The genre has recurring plots, each with a real mechanism: **the expired domain or botched registrar change** (organizational failure, technical blast radius — domain expiry monitoring is the cheapest insurance in infrastructure); **the DNSSEC misfire** (signing correctly is safety; re-signing/rotation done wrong is self-inflicted invisibility — validating resolvers treat your zone as bogus and drop it; if you run DNSSEC, automate the key ceremonies and monitor validation externally); **the resolver capacity event** (the VPC resolver's per-ENI query limits, the overwhelmed on-prem BIND — resolution *slowness* that manifests as everything-slowness, because every connection setup now carries a stalled lookup; per-instance caching and query-volume dashboards are the fixes); **the negative-cache trap** (NXDOMAIN answers are cached too — query a name *before* creating it, and clients keep saying it doesn't exist for the negative TTL; the "we created the record, why doesn't it resolve" mystery, solved); and **the delegation drift** (NS records at the parent disagreeing with the zone — works-until-it-doesn't behavior that surfaces on resolver-behavior differences). None of these is exotic; all reward the same reflex — *check resolution explicitly, from the affected vantage point, before debugging the application:* `dig` at the authoritative server (is the record right at source?), then through the resolver path (what's cached where?), comparing vantage points (is this global or local?). Minutes, not hours, once the reflex exists.

## The hygiene checklist

What mature estates do about all of the above: **DNS records live in infrastructure-as-code** (reviewed, versioned, revertible — console-edited DNS is how typos take down products, and how nobody knows what changed at 14:32); **monitoring watches resolution externally** (synthetic queries from multiple vantage points against your critical names, alerting on wrong-or-slow answers, plus domain/DNSSEC expiry checks — DNS monitoring from *inside* your own network shares fate with what it monitors); **TTL policy is deliberate** (short — 60–300s — on records that participate in failover and migration; longer on the stable; and *documented*, so the next engineer knows which records are steering wheels); **resolver architecture is designed, not inherited** (per-host/node caching, redundant recursive paths, and awareness of every cloud resolver limit in the request path); and **the runtime layer is audited once per stack** — connection-pool re-resolution behavior, language-level DNS caching, container base-image resolver quirks — because the story of most DNS failovers-that-didn't is written in the application layer, not the zone file.

DNS's whole job is to be forgettable, and everything above serves that goal: model it as a cached eventually-consistent database, respect the TTL wave, use it for the steering it's good at, learn the five failure plots, and codify the hygiene. Done, the eye-roll retires — not because it stops being DNS, but because when it is, someone knows in four minutes.
