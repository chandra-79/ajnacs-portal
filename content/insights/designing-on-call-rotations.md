On-call is where an engineering organization's stated values meet its 3 AM reality. Companies that describe themselves as sustainable, learning-oriented, and blameless will nonetheless run rotations where the same two people absorb every page, nobody remembers why half the alerts exist, and the exit interviews cluster around the phrase "I couldn't sleep." The tell is treating on-call as a *burden to distribute fairly* rather than what it actually is: a **system with design parameters** — load, structure, escalation, compensation, feedback — that produce either a sustainable practice or a slow-motion attrition engine, depending on choices someone is responsible for.

Here are those parameters, and the settings that work.

## The load budget: on-call has an SLO too

Start with the number almost nobody writes down: **how many pages per shift is acceptable?** The teams that run on-call well treat page load as a budgeted quantity with alarms on the budget — a common healthy target: a *typical* week-long shift produces a handful of pages, few or none overnight; a shift that exceeds (say) two overnight pages or a dozen total triggers not sympathy but *work*: alert review, reliability investment, or staffing decisions. Two corollaries with teeth. First, **every page must be actionable by the person paged** — if the response to an alert is "watch it" or "forward to the team that owns it," it isn't a page; it's a notification wearing a siren, and it belongs in a ticket queue or someone else's rotation. The single highest-leverage on-call improvement in most organizations remains deleting and demoting alerts (the burn-rate migration covered under SLO practice does much of this mechanically). Second, **track and publish load per shift** — pages, hours of interrupted sleep, weekend interventions — because unmeasured load distributes invisibly and unevenly, and the person silently absorbing it is usually your most conscientious engineer, right up until their resignation surprises you.

## Structure: rotations, depth, and the ownership question

**Rotation size:** six-to-eight people per rotation is the sustainable band — below five, shifts recur too often for recovery (a four-person rotation is a 25% life-tax); above ten, each engineer is on-call too rarely to stay sharp, and incident skills decay between shifts. Teams too small to staff a rotation shouldn't run one alone — merge rotations with adjacent teams over a shared service surface, or question whether the service tier justifies paging at all.

**Depth:** a primary/secondary structure with explicit escalation timers (primary has 5–10 minutes to acknowledge before secondary pages) protects against the single point of failure that is one human's phone, and doubles as the mentorship channel — new joiners ride secondary for a cycle or two before taking primary, converting on-call onboarding from hazing into apprenticeship. Beyond secondary, escalation goes to a *named* incident-command or leadership chain, not to "whoever's around."

**The ownership question — "you build it, you run it" versus a dedicated ops/SRE rotation:** the developer-on-call model has won the argument for most product services, for one decisive reason — it closes the feedback loop; the person woken by the memory leak has both the ability and now the motivation to fix it, and reliability work stops being someone else's backlog. The centralized model retains its place at platform scale (shared infrastructure with deep specialist skills) and as a *transitional* structure. What doesn't work is the worst hybrid: developers ship, a separate team gets paged, and the pain never reaches the people who can end it — that's the 1990s ops model with extra YAML.

**Follow-the-sun** is the structural luxury of genuinely distributed organizations — hand the pager across time zones and nobody owns nights. If you have engineering hubs eight-ish hours apart, building rotations around them is worth real organizational effort (shared runbooks, disciplined handoffs with a written state summary, symmetric tooling access); if you don't, resist the fiction — a lone remote engineer "covering APAC" is a single point of failure with jet lag.

## The humane mechanics

The parts that determine whether people volunteer for or flee from the rotation: **compensation honesty** — on-call constrains a person's life whether or not pages fire (can't travel, can't drink, sleep with a phone), and organizations that pay for that constraint (stipends per shift, time-off-in-lieu after disrupted nights, page-load-triggered recovery days) are stating that they know it costs something; organizations that don't are pricing it at zero and paying in turnover instead. **Shift hygiene** — trade shifts freely with a visible calendar and no manager ceremony; never schedule someone's shift against their vacation re-entry week; and after a brutal overnight incident, the *default* (not the request) is that the responder's next morning is theirs. **Tooling floor** — a page must arrive with context (alert → runbook link → dashboard → recent deploys), because the difference between a 10-minute and a 90-minute 3 AM incident is usually pre-work: runbooks that exist and are tested, access that works from a phone/laptop without a VPN scavenger hunt, and a one-command way to declare an incident and summon help. Every friction in that path is paid at the worst possible hourly rate.

## The feedback loop is the actual product

A rotation without a feedback loop is pain distribution; the loop is what converts pain into engineering. The cadence that works: **every shift ends with a handoff review** (ten minutes: what fired, what was noise, what's still smoldering) feeding a **standing on-call review** (weekly or per-cycle: page trends, top noisy alerts, stuck remediations) with **real backlog authority** — the review must be empowered to demote alerts, schedule reliability work against feature pressure, and invoke the error-budget policy when page load says the system is out of balance. Close the loop personally too: the engineer who took the 3 AM page gets first claim on fixing its root cause during business hours — closure the pager's psychology badly needs.

And the leading indicators of rot, worth watching explicitly: rising acknowledge-then-ignore rates (alert trust eroding), page load concentrating on a subset of the rotation (skill or ownership imbalance), veterans quietly trading away their shifts (the market pricing your rotation's misery before HR does), and runbook edits trending to zero (knowledge fossilizing). Each has a boring intervention, and all of them are cheaper than the senior engineer who leaves.

The reframe that pulls it together: the pager is a *product* whose users are your own engineers, and it deserves what any product gets — load budgets, UX investment, feedback cycles, and a roadmap. Teams that run it that way discover the counterintuitive endgame: a well-designed on-call rotation becomes one of the *stickiest* parts of engineering culture — the place people learn the system deepest, trust each other most, and see their fixes matter fastest. The 3 AM page never becomes pleasant. It can become rare, actionable, fairly shared, honestly paid, and guaranteed to make tomorrow's system better than today's — and that's the whole design brief.
