Every subsequent engineering hire is evaluated against the standard the first hire established. The practices they introduced, the patterns they chose, the corners they cut — these become the baseline that the team normalises. Engineers three through ten inherit the architecture the first engineer built, the testing practices they established, and the on-call culture they modelled.

This is why the first engineering hire deserves a different level of deliberation than the fifth or the tenth. The fifth engineer joins a functioning team with established practices and can adapt to them. The first engineer creates the practices.

The specific failure mode: founders hire for speed because they need to move fast, and speed is a legitimate early priority. The engineer who moves fastest is often not the engineer who makes the best long-term architectural decisions. Greenfield development is fast regardless. The places where engineering judgment compounds are in decisions about what to abstract and what to leave concrete, what to build as a service and what to embed in the monolith, how to structure data so that the system can be queried in ways you have not anticipated yet.

These decisions are made in the first six months. They shape the codebase for years. The first engineering hire is disproportionately consequential compared to any subsequent hire, and the hiring process should reflect that. Not a longer process — a more thoughtful one, focused on judgment and system-level reasoning rather than task-level output speed.
