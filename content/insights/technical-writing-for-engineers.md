Ask senior engineers what unexpectedly determined their impact and a surprising fraction name the same thing: writing. Not because prose is a soft skill adjacent to the real work, but because at scale *the writing is a control surface* — the design doc routes a quarter of engineering effort toward or away from the right build, the incident report either changes the system or gets filed, the RFC either aligns five teams or spawns the meeting series that replaces it, and the PR description either earns a real review or gets the rubber stamp that ships the bug. This series has repeatedly located leverage in written artifacts — ADRs, runbooks, postmortems, contracts, the async-collaboration substrate — and it's worth addressing the underlying skill directly, because it's a *craft* with learnable moves, not a talent you have or lack.

## The genres, because they have different jobs

Engineering writing isn't one skill but a handful, each with a shape the reader expects (the documentation-quadrant insight, generalized): **the design doc / RFC** — persuasion plus specification, whose job is to make a proposal *criticizable before it's expensive*: context and constraints first, the decision and its rationale, the alternatives *fairly* considered (the strawman-alternatives tell reveals a doc arguing rather than thinking — and reviewers smell it), the risks named by the author (naming your own weak points is what earns trust and better review, per the diligence-honesty signature). **The incident report / postmortem** — narrative plus analysis, whose job is *organizational learning*: the timeline as spine, the mechanism explained so a newcomer understands, the contributing factors traced without the blame that makes the next author defensive (the blameless discipline is a *writing* discipline as much as a cultural one), and remediation as owned commitments, not aspirations. **The PR description** — the underrated one: what changed, *why*, and how to verify — the five minutes that save each reviewer twenty and determine whether review is substantive or ceremonial (the code-review economics start here). **The status update / decision record** — compression as a service, whose job is letting readers *not* attend the meeting (the async-collaboration currency). Knowing which genre you're in — and giving the reader its expected shape — is half the craft; the design doc written as a stream of consciousness and the postmortem written as a defense both fail by ignoring what their reader came for.

## The one discipline underneath all of them

If there's a single transferable move, it's this: **write for the reader's context, not from your own.** The author knows the whole problem; the reader arrives cold, busy, and skeptical — and most bad engineering writing is the author's discovery process transcribed (the order they figured it out) instead of the reader's comprehension path (the order they need to receive it). The concrete techniques all serve this inversion: **BLUF — bottom line up front** (the conclusion, decision, or ask in the first lines; the reader who stops after the first paragraph should still get the point — the pyramid principle that respects that most readers *do* stop early), **one idea per paragraph, structured for skimming** (headers, short paragraphs, the key sentence first — because technical readers skim before they read, and writing that punishes skimming gets abandoned — the same attention-scarcity math from the context-engineering chapter, applied to human readers), **concrete over abstract** (the example before the principle; "the query scans 400M rows" over "performance concerns"), **precision as respect** (the ambiguous sentence spawns the clarifying thread that costs a day at async latency — the vague requirement is a bug with a delayed fault, per the collaboration-cost queueing), and **ruthless deletion** (the second draft's job is removing the words the first draft needed to think — length is not thoroughness, and the reader's time is the scarce resource you're spending). The craft is mostly *revision*: nobody writes the reader-first version first; they write the author-first version and then invert it, and the willingness to do that second pass is what separates writing that lands from writing that's merely correct.

## The organizational and AI-era stakes

Two forces raise the premium. **Organizationally**, writing scales where speaking doesn't: the well-written doc informs a thousand readers across time zones and years (the async substrate, the searchable commons), while the brilliant verbal explanation informs the six people in the room once — which is why remote and large organizations run on documents, and why "communicates clearly in writing" belongs in the promotion rubric for senior engineers as a *leverage* competency, not a nicety (and why teaching it — through review of writing, not just code — is an underinvested management move). **In the AI era**, the calculus sharpens counterintuitively: assistants make *drafting* nearly free — the blank-page tax, the first-pass structure, the polish — which relocates the scarce, human, valuable part to exactly where it always lived: *knowing what's true, deciding what matters, and judging what to cut.* The engineer who can't write was never really blocked on prose mechanics; they were blocked on the thinking that prose forces, and the generation tools don't do that thinking — they do the typing, which frees more attention for the judgment, which means the writing-as-thinking skill got *more* valuable, not less (the generation-versus-judgment inversion, at the level of the individual keyboard). The tools also raised the *reader's* expectations (fluent text is now free, so fluency stopped being a signal) and the premium on the things they can't fake: correctness, specificity, hard-won context, and the courage to write the honest risk section.

The closing frame, in the spirit of the series: writing is where an engineer's understanding becomes leverage — the mechanism by which one person's clear thinking routes many people's effort, and the artifact that outlives the meeting, the sprint, and the author's tenure. It's learnable (write the author's draft, then invert it for the reader; lead with the point; cut what you needed to think but they don't need to read), it compounds (every clear doc raises the baseline and trains the next writer), and it's the rare skill the AI era made *more* central by automating everything around it except the part that was always the point. The best engineers aren't necessarily the best writers — but the ones whose impact exceeds their raw skill almost always are, because they learned that the system you can't explain clearly is a system you don't yet understand, and the writing is where you find out.
