The business case for hiring experienced candidates who have already done the specific job is straightforward: shorter ramp time, immediate contribution, lower risk of mis-hire. The business case is real and limited. It does not account for availability, retention, or the compounding that comes from developing people.

Experienced candidates for specific roles exist in limited supply, particularly in specialised domains — cloud architecture, machine learning infrastructure, security engineering. Hiring exclusively from that pool creates competition for a constrained set of people and compensation pressure that compounds as those people are attractive to acquire. Retention must be evaluated in the organization itself; prior experience alone does not establish how long someone will stay.

The teams that compound are built partly on experienced anchors and partly on people who are growing into the role. The growing-into people require more investment upfront. They can build valuable knowledge of the organization over time when the role offers effective support and meaningful work.

The hiring skill this requires: assessing learning velocity and reasoning quality rather than experience inventory. Standard engineering interviews are better designed for the latter — they check whether a candidate has already seen the problems the role involves. Assessing learning velocity requires giving candidates genuinely novel problems with insufficient information and watching how they handle the gap. Assessing reasoning quality requires asking not just what they would do but why, and following the thread far enough to understand whether the reasoning is principled or pattern-matched.

Both are learnable interview skills. Most engineering organisations do not invest in learning them.

## Define what can be learned after joining

Potential is not a substitute for the requirements of the role. Separate skills needed on arrival from those the team can teach within a supported ramp. An engineer who will be the only production responder needs different prior evidence from a developer joining a staffed team with a mentor. List the first three meaningful tasks, the risks of a mistake and the assistance available. Use those tasks to design the assessment instead of making a general judgment about whether someone seems talented.

A learning exercise should provide information, allow questions and observe how the candidate updates a solution. It should not reward guessing the interviewer's preferred answer. Give every candidate the same initial problem, comparable hints and a common scoring rubric. Record evidence before the panel discusses the person. Familiarity with the interviewer, confidence of presentation and experience with the exact puzzle can otherwise dominate the decision.

## Include coaching in the capacity calculation

Imagine a fictional team deciding whether it can support two engineers entering a new domain. Each requires two hours of paired learning and one hour of review per week for six weeks. The planned mentor commitment is 2×3×6=36 hours. A schedule that budgets only one hour per person each week reserves 12 hours and leaves a 24-hour gap. Neither amount predicts the actual ramp; the exercise exposes whether the advertised support exists in the plan.

Record preparation and interrupt time as well. Pairing for two hours does not include the time spent creating a safe dataset or arranging access. Conversely, an effective shared exercise may serve both learners without doubling every minute of preparation. Use ranges and revisit the plan after observing the first tasks. Do not interpret slower initial output as evidence that the hiring decision failed when the promised environment was unavailable.

## A structured assessment exercise

Present a small service whose response time has increased. Supply a diagram, a few measurements and an incomplete hypothesis. Ask the candidate what they would inspect first and why. After they answer, provide a new observation that contradicts one plausible explanation. Score the ability to identify uncertainty, revise the model, respect operational boundaries and explain the next step. Those dimensions should correspond to the work the candidate would actually do.

Use separate rubric anchors. A weak response might recommend a production change without validating the symptom. A useful response may ask for a comparison period and isolate the affected path. A strong response can explain what observation would falsify its leading hypothesis. These are examples to calibrate with the team, not a universal hiring standard. Make reasonable accommodations and provide an equivalent way to demonstrate the skill when the format introduces avoidable barriers.

## Validate the process after hiring

Compare the assessment evidence with later work, using appropriate privacy controls and a defined review period. Did the interview reveal the capabilities the role actually needed? Did it reject candidates because of trivia that never appeared in the job? Discuss mismatches with the panel and update the exercise. A small number of hires cannot establish statistical predictive validity, but it can reveal obvious problems in the rubric or onboarding plan.

Avoid unsupported assumptions about retention. Experienced lateral hires are not inherently less loyal, and less-experienced hires do not automatically outperform them over several years. Compensation, opportunity, management and personal circumstances all matter. A balanced team can include people with different experience profiles when the hiring evidence and available support justify those choices. The operational commitment is to make the promised development opportunity real.
