The business case for hiring experienced candidates who have already done the specific job is straightforward: shorter ramp time, immediate contribution, lower risk of mis-hire. The business case is real and limited. It does not account for availability, retention, or the compounding that comes from developing people.

Experienced candidates for specific roles exist in limited supply, particularly in specialised domains — cloud architecture, machine learning infrastructure, security engineering. Hiring exclusively from that pool creates competition for a constrained set of people and compensation pressure that compounds as those people are attractive to acquire. The team built entirely from experienced external hires also tends toward lower retention because the engineers who seek that kind of move are often seeking novelty, and novelty has a shelf life.

The teams that compound are built partly on experienced anchors and partly on people who are growing into the role. The growing-into people require more investment upfront. They produce distinctive results over a two-to-three-year horizon that the experienced lateral hire often does not, because they have built their expertise inside your system, your culture, and your specific challenges rather than importing expertise from elsewhere.

The hiring skill this requires: assessing learning velocity and reasoning quality rather than experience inventory. Standard engineering interviews are better designed for the latter — they check whether a candidate has already seen the problems the role involves. Assessing learning velocity requires giving candidates genuinely novel problems with insufficient information and watching how they handle the gap. Assessing reasoning quality requires asking not just what they would do but why, and following the thread far enough to understand whether the reasoning is principled or pattern-matched.

Both are learnable interview skills. Most engineering organisations do not invest in learning them.
