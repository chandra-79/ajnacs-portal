Usage-based pricing ate SaaS — per-seat gave way to per-request, per-gigabyte, per-token — and it quietly converted billing from a CRUD feature into one of the estate's most demanding data systems: a pipeline that must capture every billable event (missed events are *revenue leakage*, silent and compounding), never double-count (duplicates are *customer disputes* with churn attached), aggregate correctly across time zones and plan changes, and produce numbers that finance can reconcile and auditors can trace. The invoice is the one report where "approximately right" is wrong in both directions — and the engineering that makes it exactly right is a tour of this series' disciplines with money attached.

## Capture: the metering event as a financial record

The foundational decision is treating usage events as **immutable financial facts** with the full event-discipline treatment: a unique event ID (the idempotency key that everything downstream dedupes on — at-least-once delivery is the weather here as everywhere, and a metering pipeline without dedup is a double-billing generator on every retry), a timestamp *of the usage* (event time, not processing time — the watermark disciplines apply because late-arriving events are routine, and "which invoice does 11:59 PM month-end usage land on" is a question with legal stakes; declare the late-event policy — a grace window, then next period — and encode it, don't improvise it per incident), the tenant and resource dimensions (the multi-tenant context propagation, load-bearing), and the quantity in *raw, unrated units* (meter what happened; price it later — the separation that makes repricing, plan changes, and disputes tractable).

Capture placement follows the trust gradient: **meter at the boundary you control** (the gateway/service layer emitting events transactionally with the work — the outbox pattern earning its keep, because "the API call succeeded but the metering event was lost" is exactly the dual-write bug, now denominated in dollars), never from client-side reporting, and with **completeness monitoring from day one** (the absence-detection disciplines: expected-volume checks against operational metrics — if the API served 10M requests and the meter recorded 9.2M, that 8% is either an incident or a discount you didn't intend to give).

## Aggregation and rating: the pipeline with an audit trail

The pipeline shape is familiar — stream or batch aggregation from raw events to per-tenant, per-meter, per-period totals — with billing-specific hardening: **replayability as a legal requirement** (the raw event log retained and reprocessable — the CDC/streaming rebuild superpower, here because disputes and bugs demand "recompute March from source" as a routine operation, and because restatement-with-explanation is the trust-preserving move from data-quality practice), **exactly-once *effects* into the billing store** (the transactional-sink disciplines — aggregation reruns must converge, not double), **period-close semantics** (a deliberate close-the-books step per period — late events after close flow to the next invoice per the declared policy; mutable "final" numbers are how engineering and finance stop trusting each other), and **plan-aware rating as a separate, versioned stage** (the rating engine applies pricing — tiers, included quantities, overage rates, credits — to metered totals; pricing rules are *versioned configuration with effective dates*, because "the price changed mid-month" and "the customer upgraded on the 12th" are the routine cases, and proration logic hand-rolled per occasion is the bug farm; this stage is also where the buy-vs-build line usually sits — the metering pipeline is yours because it's welded to your architecture, while rating/invoicing/dunning is a product category the build-vs-buy framework treats kindly).

**Reconciliation is the discipline that makes finance a stakeholder instead of an adversary**: metered totals tied to operational metrics (the completeness check above), rated invoices tied to recognized revenue, and cash tied to invoices — each a scheduled, alerting comparison (the reconciliation-loop pattern, now closing the books), with discrepancies triaged by an owner. The estates that skip this run an annual ritual called "the audit finding."

## The product surface: usage visibility as churn prevention

The billing system's user-facing half carries disproportionate product weight: **real-time usage visibility** (dashboards and APIs showing consumption-to-date against plan — served from the streaming aggregation tier, clearly labeled with its staleness budget per the honesty disciplines, because "why is my dashboard behind my invoice" is a ticket category to design away), **alerts before surprises** (threshold notifications — 80% of plan, projected overage — the burn-rate alerting concept pointed at the customer's wallet; the surprise invoice is the single most churn-correlated event in usage-based SaaS, and it's entirely preventable engineering), **spending caps as a product feature** (hard limits that degrade service rather than accrue charges, for the customers who want them — the load-shedding contract, chosen by the buyer), and **the invoice that explains itself** (line items traceable to meters, drill-down from invoice to daily usage — the correlation-ID philosophy applied to money, because a disputable invoice that can't be decomposed *will* be disputed, and the support cost lands on engineering anyway).

The closing calibration for the architecture review: metering is the rare pipeline where the data-engineering disciplines — event-time correctness, idempotent processing, replayable history, reconciled outputs — stop being best practices and become fiduciary requirements, and where the cost of retrofitting rigor (re-deriving months of usage from logs that weren't designed as financial records) is among the most painful projects in SaaS. Build the meter like the financial system it is from the first billable event: immutable dedupable facts at the source, versioned rating, closed periods, reconciled books, and a customer-facing surface that makes consumption legible before it becomes contested. Revenue integrity, it turns out, is just data integrity that finance reads — and they read it every month, whether or not engineering designed for the audience.
