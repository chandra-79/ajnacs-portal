The expired-certificate outage is the industry's most embarrassing recurring incident: fully predictable (the expiry date was known years in advance), fully preventable (renewal is mechanical), and yet it keeps taking down major services — because certificate management at enterprise scale is an *inventory and automation* problem being solved with calendars and heroics. The era of tolerating that is ending on a schedule: browser-mandated maximum certificate lifetimes are contracting stepwise toward **47 days by 2029**, which converts "renew annually, carefully" into "renew constantly, automatically" — manual certificate operations are being regulated out of existence. Here's the discipline that replaces them.

## The model: certificates are short-lived credentials, not installed artifacts

The mental shift that everything else follows from: stop treating a certificate as a *thing you install* and start treating it as a *credential that rotates* — like the short-lived tokens of modern IAM practice, just at the transport layer. A certificate is a signed binding of a key to a name, valid briefly, renewed mechanically, deployed without human hands. Systems designed around that assumption (automated issuance, hot-reload of certs, no deploy required to rotate) are immune to the entire outage genre; systems where a renewal means a change ticket and a maintenance window are accumulating scheduled incidents.

**ACME is the protocol that industrialized this** — the Let's Encrypt-popularized issue/renew automation now supported by essentially every serious CA, public and private. The architecture it enables: certificates requested and renewed by software (cert-manager in Kubernetes, ACME clients on hosts, the load balancer's or CDN's built-in managed certificates — the "certificate as a checkbox" tier that should carry as much of your public surface as possible), validated by DNS or HTTP challenges (DNS-01 via your IaC-managed zones for wildcards and non-web services), renewed at two-thirds of lifetime with alerting on *failure to renew* — the alarm that matters, firing weeks before impact while the fix is trivial.

## The inventory problem: you cannot rotate what you don't know exists

Every certificate-outage postmortem contains the same sentence: "this certificate wasn't in the tracking system." The unglamorous core of the discipline is a **complete, continuously refreshed certificate inventory** — built not from what teams register (they won't) but from *discovery*: scanning your IP space and DNS zones for listening TLS endpoints, querying cloud APIs for provisioned certs (load balancers, CDNs, API gateways), and — the highest-leverage source most estates ignore — **Certificate Transparency logs**, where every publicly-issued certificate for your domains appears whether or not you knew it was requested. CT monitoring simultaneously feeds the inventory and functions as a security control (an unexpected certificate for your domain is either shadow IT or an attack, and both deserve a page).

The inventory's schema is the operational payload: what's the cert, where does it terminate, who owns it, how does it renew (which automation, or the dreaded "manually"), and when does it expire — with the "manual" rows constituting the burn-down backlog and expiry-within-30-days-without-automation constituting the standing alarm. The long tail is where the outages live: the appliance UI, the partner SFTP endpoint, the SaaS integration that pinned your cert, the internal tool on a VM from 2019 — none glamorous, all capable of a sev-2.

## Internal PKI: run it like a product or buy it

Service-to-service TLS (and especially mTLS) needs an internal certificate authority, and internal PKI is where good intentions produce five-year root certs on a laptop. The design that works: a **root CA kept offline** (or in an HSM/KMS) signing **intermediate CAs that do the daily work**, short-lived end-entity certs (days, not years — internal certs have no browser constraints, so run them *shorter* than public ones; rotation exercises your automation continuously and shrinks compromise windows to irrelevance), and issuance through a real platform — cert-manager against a private CA (the cloud providers all sell one), HashiCorp Vault's PKI engine, or — for mesh estates — the **service mesh's identity machinery** (SPIFFE/SPIRE-class workload identity, Istio's built-in CA), which issues per-workload certificates bound to platform-verified identity and rotates them hourly without any human ever seeing a key. That last tier is the destination: mTLS everywhere, certificates as invisible plumbing, identity anchored in the platform rather than in files.

The governance layer that must exist regardless: documented chain-of-trust (what signs what, and what would revoking each layer cost), tested **CA rotation runbooks** (intermediates *will* need replacing; estates that have never rotated an intermediate own a future incident with a countdown), constrained issuance (name constraints and policy on what each intermediate may sign — an unconstrained internal CA trusted by every workstation is a domain-wide compromise amplifier), and distribution discipline for trust stores (the *other* half of rotation: getting new roots *trusted* everywhere before old ones retire — the slow part, budgeted first).

## The operational rim

The remaining practices, compactly: **hot-reload as a requirement** — every TLS terminator in the estate (proxies, services, appliances) must pick up renewed certs without restart, and the ones that can't get fronted by something that can; **monitoring that watches the endpoint, not the file** — external synthetic checks validating the *served* chain and its expiry from the client's viewpoint (the renewed-but-not-deployed cert is the second-most-common outage plot); **key hygiene** as code review policy — keys generated where they'll live, never emailed, never in git (secret scanners catch the sins), algorithm currency (ECDSA as sane default) tracked estate-wide via the inventory; and **the post-quantum horizon** wired into planning rather than panic — TLS key exchange is already going hybrid post-quantum at the big providers, certificate signatures will follow as standards and CA support mature, and the estates that will absorb that transition cheaply are precisely the ones this article describes: full inventory, automated issuance, short lifetimes, rotation as routine. Crypto-agility, once again, is mostly the compounding interest on boring operational hygiene.

The end state to hold as the target: no human knows any certificate's expiry date, because no certificate's renewal depends on a human — issuance is platform machinery, rotation is continuous, the inventory refreshes itself, and the only alerts are "automation failed, fix at leisure" rather than "handshake failing, fix at 3 AM." The 47-day future makes this posture mandatory; the outage genre it retires makes it worthwhile years before the mandate arrives.
