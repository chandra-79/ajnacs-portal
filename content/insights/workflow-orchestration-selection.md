"We need workflow orchestration" is a sentence that launches a hundred mismatched tool evaluations, because it names a symptom — multi-step processes duct-taped together with cron, queues, and prayer — rather than a workload. The orchestration market is really **three distinct tool families for three distinct job shapes**, and most selection pain comes from evaluating across families: comparing Airflow to Temporal is comparing a dishwasher to a washing machine because both involve water. Here's the taxonomy, the representative tools, and the placement rules.

## Family one: batch/data orchestrators (Airflow and descendants)

The shape: **scheduled DAGs of coarse-grained tasks over data** — extract, transform, load, validate, publish; hourly and nightly rhythms; tasks that are containers or queries taking minutes; success meaning "the data landed correctly." Apache Airflow remains the incumbent vocabulary here, with Dagster and Prefect as the modern refinements (Dagster's asset-oriented model — declaring the *data assets* and deriving the pipeline — being the significant conceptual upgrade: orchestrating *what should exist* rather than *what should run*, which is where the data world has been heading).

What this family is good at: scheduling with calendars and sensors, backfills (rerun last March after the logic fix — a first-class, load-bearing operation), dependency visualization, retry-with-alerting on task granularity, and the operational sociology of data teams (analysts reading DAG UIs). What it's *not*: low-latency (schedulers tick in seconds-to-minutes), high-frequency (thousands of runs/sec is not the design point), or transactional (compensation logic and exactly-once side effects are your problem, per the usual idempotency disciplines). The classic misuse: putting order processing or payment flows into Airflow because it was already installed — request-driven business transactions in a batch scheduler inherit batch latency and none of the transactional guarantees they need.

## Family two: durable execution (Temporal and kin)

The shape: **long-running, failure-sensitive business processes** — order fulfillment, onboarding, payment orchestration, infrastructure provisioning; triggered by events, not calendars; running seconds to months; where the hard requirement is *the process must make progress exactly-once-ish despite every component failing*. Temporal (and the durable-execution family around it — Cadence lineage, cloud offerings, newer entrants) solves this with a genuinely different architecture: **workflow-as-code with replay** — your workflow is ordinary code whose every step and timer is journaled; when a worker dies, another replays the history and continues as if nothing happened. State management, retries, timers ("wait 30 days, then..."), and the saga compensations discussed elsewhere become *language-native constructs* instead of hand-built state machines in database tables.

The price: the **determinism contract** (workflow code must replay identically — no direct I/O, clocks, or randomness in workflow logic; side effects live in activities — a real discipline with real code-review implications and a learning curve teams consistently underestimate), the operational platform itself (self-hosted Temporal is a serious stateful system; the managed cloud is the sane default), and versioning of in-flight workflows (long-running instances spanning deploys need explicit change-management — solvable, standard, but a discipline the cron job never asked of you). The payoff, equally real: the entire genre of "stuck order" incidents — half-completed processes with no record of where they died — is architecturally deleted, replaced by queryable, resumable, observable process instances.

## Family three: cloud-native state machines (Step Functions and peers)

The shape: **glue between managed services** — the S3-upload-triggers-processing pipeline, the approval flow chaining Lambdas and queues, the ML batch job coordinating cloud services. Step Functions (and Azure/GCP equivalents) define workflows as declarative state machines (JSON/YAML), integrate natively with the provider's service catalog (direct service invocations without wrapper code), and bill per transition with serverless operational cost — nothing to run, nothing to patch.

Strengths: zero-ops, deep provider integration, honest durability, and drag-and-drop legibility for simple flows. Limits: expressiveness (complex branching and data manipulation in declarative JSON becomes write-only origami — the sign you've outgrown the family), portability (the definition *is* the vendor), testing ergonomics (local simulation exists but the feedback loop trails code-native approaches), and cost at very high volume (per-transition pricing compounds). The placement rule: brilliant for cloud-service choreography of modest complexity; wrong for rich business logic, which wants family two.

## The selection framework, and the estate reality

Three questions place almost any workload. **What triggers it?** Calendar/data-arrival → family one; business event/API call → family two or three. **What's the failure requirement?** "Rerun the batch" → family one; "must resume mid-process with compensations" → family two; "retry the step" → any. **Where does the logic live?** In data transformations → one; in code with complex business rules → two; in wiring between managed services → three.

And the estate-level truths that outrank tool zeal: **mature estates legitimately run one of each** (the data platform on Airflow/Dagster, transactional processes on Temporal-class, cloud glue on Step Functions) — consolidation across families buys contortion, not simplicity (the *within*-family sprawl — four Airflow instances, say — is what deserves consolidation). **The workflow layer is production infrastructure, not scaffolding**: it needs the same observability (per-run tracing, stuck-run alerting by age — the "no workflow silently stalls" invariant), the same CI (workflow definitions are code: reviewed, tested, versioned), and the same security posture (orchestrators hold credentials to *everything* they touch — they're privileged-automation surfaces per the IAM disciplines, and a compromised orchestrator is a compromised estate). And **don't build family two yourself**: the hand-rolled workflow engine — a status column, a poller, a growing pile of edge cases — is among the most-repeated infrastructure mistakes of the past decade; the moment a process needs durable multi-step state with compensation, the build-vs-buy answer is known, and it's buy (or adopt open source with the managed control plane).

The compact placement rule to leave in the design review: **data on schedules → Airflow-family; money and state machines in code → Temporal-family; cloud-service wiring → Step Functions-family** — with idempotent steps everywhere, because whichever family runs your process, at-least-once execution remains the universal weather.
