The phrase "hiring for potential" gets used in two very different ways. One is a genuine philosophy about how to build a team that grows over time. The other is a rationalization for hiring under-leveled candidates to save money.

Distinguishing between them matters because the genuine approach is one of the highest-leverage things an engineering team can do, and the rationalization version is expensive.

## What genuine potential looks like

Potential is the rate at which a person grows, applied to the gap between where they are and where they could be. A candidate with five years of experience at a senior level has demonstrated a certain ceiling. A candidate with two years of experience who is already performing at a mid-level and whose trajectory is clearly upward may have a much higher ceiling — you are just earlier on the curve.

The indicators of high potential that are reliably observable in an interview:

**Learning velocity, not just learning.** The candidate who can describe something they did not know six months ago, explain how they learned it, and apply it with competence is demonstrating something more useful than the candidate who already knew it. The learning mechanism is more valuable than the current knowledge state.

**Calibration under uncertainty.** How does the candidate respond when they do not know something? The highly calibrated engineer says "I don't know, but I would approach it by..." The overconfident engineer guesses without flagging uncertainty. The underconfident engineer refuses to engage. Calibration is a strong predictor of long-term performance.

**How they talk about their mistakes.** The candidate who can describe a technical decision they got wrong, explain what they misunderstood, and articulate what they would do differently has the self-awareness that enables growth. This is rarer than it sounds.

**Intellectual curiosity beyond the resume.** What do they learn about because they want to, not because a job required it? The engineer who reads systems design papers for enjoyment, who experiments with new languages on weekends, who asks thoughtful questions about the interviewer's work — this person's growth trajectory is self-fueled.

## Why it compounds

A team built entirely from already-experienced engineers produces an engineering culture that matches the level of experience those engineers brought. A team built from high-potential engineers that invests in developing them produces engineers who grow to levels above what any of them started with — and the culture compounds.

The mid-level engineer who becomes senior on your team is more valuable than the senior engineer you hired, because they understand the system from its foundation, they are invested in it, and they will stay longer.

Hire people who are going somewhere, not just people who have been somewhere.
