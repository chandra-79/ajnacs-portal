The phrase "hiring for potential" gets used in two very different ways. One is a genuine philosophy about how to build a team that grows over time. The other is a rationalization for hiring under-leveled candidates to save money.

Distinguishing between them matters because the genuine approach is one of the highest-leverage things an engineering team can do, and the rationalization version is expensive.

## What genuine potential looks like

Potential is the rate at which a person grows, applied to the gap between where they are and where they could be. A candidate with five years of senior-level experience has demonstrated capability in particular contexts, not a fixed ceiling. A candidate with two years of experience who is already performing at a mid-level and whose trajectory is clearly upward may have a much higher ceiling — you are just earlier on the curve.

Evidence worth examining through a structured, job-relevant process includes:

**Learning velocity, not just learning.** The candidate who can describe something they did not know six months ago, explain how they learned it, and apply it with competence is demonstrating something more useful than the candidate who already knew it. The learning mechanism is more valuable than the current knowledge state.

**Calibration under uncertainty.** How does the candidate respond when they do not know something? The highly calibrated engineer says "I don't know, but I would approach it by..." The overconfident engineer guesses without flagging uncertainty. The underconfident engineer refuses to engage. Calibration can be relevant to the role, but a brief interview observation does not establish a long-term performance prediction.

**How they talk about their mistakes.** The candidate who can describe a technical decision they got wrong, explain what they misunderstood, and articulate what they would do differently has the self-awareness that enables growth. This is rarer than it sounds.

**Intellectual curiosity beyond the resume.** What do they learn about because they want to, not because a job required it? The engineer who reads systems design papers for enjoyment, who experiments with new languages on weekends, who asks thoughtful questions about the interviewer's work — this person's growth trajectory is self-fueled.

## Why it compounds

Both experienced and developing engineers can learn and contribute to a team. Hiring should match the role, available support, and demonstrated capabilities. Investment in learning can be useful across experience levels, but team composition alone does not establish a future culture or guarantee growth.

An engineer who develops on the team can build valuable local knowledge; experienced hires can also contribute and remain committed. Evaluate the actual contribution rather than assuming one route is inherently superior.

Hire people who are going somewhere, not just people who have been somewhere.

## Replace a prediction of someone's ceiling with observable evidence

A short interview cannot reveal a person's ultimate capability or career trajectory. Years of experience do not establish a ceiling, and enthusiasm for weekend projects is not a fair proxy for curiosity. People have different opportunities, responsibilities and access to unpaid practice. Assess the capabilities required for the role through evidence that candidates can reasonably provide in a consistent process.

A learning task can examine how a candidate uses new information. Give a small problem, explain the unfamiliar concept needed to solve it and allow clarifying questions. Then change one assumption and ask the candidate to adapt. Score the reasoning against job-relevant criteria, not the speed with which they resemble the interviewer's preferred style.

## A fictional rubric-calibration exercise

Suppose two interviewers score six sample responses independently. They initially agree on the rubric category for three. After discussing the anchors and rescoring a different set of six, they agree on five. The chart compares agreement counts. It does not establish that the rubric predicts job performance or that the interview is unbiased; it only indicates that the raters are applying the categories more consistently in this exercise.

Inspect the disagreements rather than averaging them away. One interviewer may reward the final answer while another rewards the explanation, or they may interpret a request for clarification differently. Rewrite the rubric so each dimension has an observable anchor. Keep independent notes before panel discussion to reduce the chance that the most confident interviewer determines everyone's interpretation.

## Connect hiring to the support the role provides

For each skill that can be learned after joining, identify the first practice task, reviewer and available time. A candidate should not be hired into a developmental role that has no one able to teach or review the work. Conversely, do not label a role developmental merely to justify lower compensation for responsibilities that require immediate independent expertise.

Use the same expectations when evaluating the early ramp. If access, documentation or mentorship arrives late, record that constraint. It affects what the engineer had an opportunity to demonstrate. Review the plan jointly and revise it based on actual work rather than treating the initial hiring forecast as an unchangeable judgment about the person.

## Learn from the hiring process without overclaiming

After an appropriate period, compare interview evidence with the job's real demands. Did the exercise resemble the work? Did the rubric capture an important capability? Did an irrelevant requirement exclude otherwise suitable candidates? Small samples are useful for discovering process defects but are not enough to establish broad predictive validity.

Avoid unsupported claims that internally developed engineers will stay longer or contribute more than experienced hires. Either can thrive or struggle depending on the role and environment. A defensible hiring decision combines current capability, evidence of learning, job requirements and the support the organization can actually provide. Potential is a reason to investigate thoughtfully, not permission to make confident predictions about a person's future.
