Build-vs-buy is the decision genre where enterprises burn the most money with the least process. A million-dollar infrastructure purchase gets procurement review, security assessment, and three quotes; a team's quiet decision to *build* the same capability — committing a decade of maintenance, on-call, and opportunity cost — often gets a sprint-planning conversation. The reverse failure is equally routine: capabilities bought because a demo was slick, then buried under two years of customization that cost more than building would have. Both failures share a root: the decision was made on instinct — engineer pride ("we could build this better") or executive fatigue ("just buy something") — rather than on the handful of questions that actually determine the answer.

## The first question: is this core or context?

The strategic filter that should precede all spreadsheet math: **does this capability differentiate us to customers, or does it merely need to work?** Core — the capability your customers choose you for, where being better *is* the business — justifies building, because owning its evolution is owning your differentiation, and renting your differentiation from a vendor who sells it to your competitors is strategy malpractice. Context — everything that must work but wins you nothing by being special: auth, billing infrastructure, CRM, observability, CI, the ticketing system — defaults hard to buy, because every engineer-hour spent rebuilding commodity capability is an hour taken from the differentiating work, and the *opportunity cost is the real cost*.

The filter's power is in how rarely teams apply it honestly. The gravitational pull runs both ways: engineers systematically misclassify context as core ("our deployment needs are unique" — they aren't; the uniqueness is usually accumulated accident, not requirement), and executives misclassify core as context ("isn't search just a product you buy?" — not when relevance is your marketplace's whole experience). A useful forcing question: *if a competitor had a visibly better version of this, would customers leave?* If no, it's context. Write the classification down; it settles half the argument before the argument.

## The honest cost model (both sides lie by default)

**Build costs are underestimated by the construction fallacy:** the estimate covers version one, but the commitment is a *product lifetime* — maintenance (the well-worn multiple of initial build cost, compounding annually), operations and on-call, security patching and compliance evidence, documentation and onboarding every new team member into the bespoke thing, and the succession risk when its two authors leave (the bus-factor line item nobody prices). The honest build estimate is initial cost × a lifetime multiplier that most organizations' own history says is 3–5×, *plus* the permanent claim on your scarcest resource — senior engineering attention.

**Buy costs are underestimated by the sticker fallacy:** license fees are the visible fraction, under which sit the **integration tax** (connecting the product to your identity, data, workflows — routinely exceeding year-one license cost, and recurring at every major upgrade), customization drift (every configuration beyond vanilla is quasi-code you maintain without owning), the vendor-management overhead (contracts, security reviews, roadmap dependence), per-seat/usage growth (the pricing curve at 3× your current scale is the number to negotiate *now*, not at renewal), and **exit cost** — the migration you'd pay to leave, which is the true price of lock-in and should be estimated *at purchase*, because it's the number that determines your renewal-negotiation leverage forever after.

Run both sides over the same horizon (five years is honest for infrastructure) with opportunity cost explicit — "build" proposals compete against the features the same engineers would otherwise ship — and most contested decisions stop being contested.

## The forgotten middle, and the modifiers

The binary framing hides where the best answers usually live: **open source adopted with operational ownership** (build-grade control at buy-grade start speed — priced honestly: you're buying a codebase and committing to operate it, the "free" that costs an SRE); **open core with managed control planes** (the pattern that has eaten infrastructure: Kafka/Temporal/Elastic-class systems run by vendors — often the rational midpoint for the operate-vs-differentiate trade); **buy-then-extend** (vanilla product, differentiation built *at its extension points* — healthy exactly as long as the extensions respect the product's upgrade path; the graveyard is "customized beyond upgradability," the worst of both worlds); and **build-on-primitives** (composing cloud services with thin custom logic — most modern "build" is actually this, and it changes the maintenance math substantially versus build-from-scratch).

Then the modifiers that legitimately bend the default: **data gravity and sovereignty** (capabilities entangled with your most sensitive data raise buy-side diligence and sometimes force build/self-host); **vendor viability** (buying context from a fragile startup imports their mortality into your stack — the acquisition-then-deprecation arc is common enough to price); **volume economics at scale** (per-usage pricing that's rational at your size and ruinous at 10× is a scheduled future build decision — better made deliberately now); and **the talent question asked both ways** (can we staff the build's *decade*? and: does the buy leave us competent enough to ever leave?).

## The decision as a process, not an event

The lightweight governance that separates mature organizations: build-vs-buy calls above a threshold get a **written one-page case** (core-or-context classification, five-year both-sides cost, exit estimate, the middle options considered) reviewed by someone with portfolio view — because the pattern failures are portfolio failures: five teams severally building the same context capability, or an estate quietly accumulating forty SaaS products with overlapping functions and no owner (the rationalization audit that finds them is reliably the fastest cost win in enterprise IT). And every decision gets a **revisit trigger** — scale thresholds, renewal dates, vendor-health events — because the answer is time-indexed: the market commoditizes yesterday's build (the thing you rightly built in 2019 may be a product now — retiring your bespoke version is a *victory*, not an admission), and your scale can un-commoditize yesterday's buy.

The summary that fits in the exec review: classify core-versus-context first and let it set the default; cost both sides over five years with maintenance multipliers, integration tax, opportunity cost, and exit priced in; check the middle options before the binary; document, portfolio-review, and revisit on triggers. The goal isn't to build less or buy less — it's to spend your engineers where they differentiate you, your money where the market has already done the work, and your decision-making effort in proportion to the decade-long commitments these choices actually are.
