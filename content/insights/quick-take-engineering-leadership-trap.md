A newly promoted engineering manager sits down with their team for the first 1-on-1. They run the meeting the way their manager used to run it. They give feedback the way they received feedback. They prioritize the team's work the way their work was prioritized.

Some of what they replicate is good. Some of it is exactly what they swore they would never do.

## Why the pattern is so persistent

Management is learned primarily by observation. Before becoming a manager, most engineers have years of experience being managed — watching how decisions are made, how feedback is delivered, how priorities are set, how conflict is handled.

When you do not have an explicit framework for how to manage, you default to the template you have seen most often, which is the management you experienced. This template includes the approaches that worked and the approaches that did not.

The manager who was micromanaged often either micromanages their own team (replicating the only template they have) or swings to the opposite extreme and under-manages (reacting against the template). Neither is deliberate. Both are the result of not examining the inherited template.

## The specific traps

**Protecting your team from information.** A manager who was kept in the dark about organizational decisions replicates this, telling themselves they are protecting their engineers from distraction. What they are doing is removing context that engineers need to make good decisions and feel invested in the work.

**Equating presence with performance.** A manager who was evaluated on hours worked evaluates their team the same way, even when output and hours are not correlated. Hours alone do not establish the quality, impact or sustainability of engineering work.

**Avoiding the difficult conversation.** A manager who was given indirect feedback (or no feedback) until a formal review replicates this by avoiding the real-time directness that allows people to course-correct while it still matters.

**Solving problems rather than developing the person.** A manager who was never coached — just directed — directs rather than coaches. Problems get fixed faster in the short term. Engineers do not develop the judgment to fix them independently.

## Breaking the template

Breaking the inherited template requires examining it explicitly. What was useful in how you were managed? What cost you? What patterns are you replicating unconsciously?

A useful management practice is to answer those questions with concrete examples. Not perfectly — nobody manages perfectly — but with enough self-awareness to interrupt the patterns they did not choose.

## Turn a management belief into a testable agreement

Choose one inherited habit, such as requiring daily status reports or personally approving every technical decision. Ask what problem it is intended to solve and whether the team currently has that problem. Then describe a smaller agreement that preserves the needed information or control. A daily written update may help a distributed project during a risky migration; the same requirement may add little value to a stable team with a visible work board.

Discuss the change with the people affected. Do not assume that everyone wants the same level of autonomy or feedback. A colleague learning a new domain may need more frequent review than an experienced owner handling familiar work. Agree on a review point and an escalation route so autonomy does not mean being left alone with an unclear expectation.

## A fictional approval-bottleneck example

A manager reviews 15 routine decisions per week, spending 12 minutes on each. That is 180 minutes. After documenting a decision boundary and delegating suitable cases, the manager reviews five exceptions at the same 12 minutes each, or 60 minutes. The chart compares review time, while the worksheet should also record any rework, missed risks and additional coaching. The apparent 120-minute release is not automatically a net team saving.

The delegated engineer now spends time making the decision, and some cases may require a new discussion. That can still be worthwhile because it develops capability and removes waiting, but the benefit should be stated accurately. If decisions become less safe or the manager silently rechecks everything, the delegation mechanism needs revision.

## Practice specific feedback

Describe an observed behavior, its effect on the work and the change you would like to discuss. For example, a design review may have ended without recording the remaining uncertainty, leaving another team unable to proceed. Ask the engineer how they understood the situation before recommending a solution. There may be a missing decision owner or conflicting instruction that the manager can resolve.

Avoid labels such as “not strategic” or “not senior enough” without examples. They give the recipient little basis for action and can hide disagreement about the role itself. Agree on a concrete next opportunity to practice the behavior and how both people will know whether it improved. Feedback should arrive early enough to help, rather than appearing for the first time in a formal evaluation.

## Examine the manager's own system of work

Look at where decisions wait for you, where team members need context you have not shared and where you are doing work that could be a supported learning opportunity. Ask peers and direct reports for examples, while making it safe to disagree. A survey score alone is unlikely to explain which behavior needs to change.

Retain habits that serve a clear purpose and revise those that do not. The aim is not to reject everything learned from earlier managers. It is to choose deliberately for this team and situation. Good management can combine technical perspective, process clarity, coaching and organizational support; none of those contributions becomes unnecessary simply because the manager is no longer the strongest specialist in every domain.
