A newly promoted engineering manager sits down with their team for the first 1-on-1. They run the meeting the way their manager used to run it. They give feedback the way they received feedback. They prioritize the team's work the way their work was prioritized.

Some of what they replicate is good. Some of it is exactly what they swore they would never do.

## Why the pattern is so persistent

Management is learned primarily by observation. Before becoming a manager, most engineers have years of experience being managed — watching how decisions are made, how feedback is delivered, how priorities are set, how conflict is handled.

When you do not have an explicit framework for how to manage, you default to the template you have seen most often, which is the management you experienced. This template includes the approaches that worked and the approaches that did not.

The manager who was micromanaged often either micromanages their own team (replicating the only template they have) or swings to the opposite extreme and under-manages (reacting against the template). Neither is deliberate. Both are the result of not examining the inherited template.

## The specific traps

**Protecting your team from information.** A manager who was kept in the dark about organizational decisions replicates this, telling themselves they are protecting their engineers from distraction. What they are doing is removing context that engineers need to make good decisions and feel invested in the work.

**Equating presence with performance.** A manager who was evaluated on hours worked evaluates their team the same way, even when output and hours are not correlated. In engineering, they are rarely correlated.

**Avoiding the difficult conversation.** A manager who was given indirect feedback (or no feedback) until a formal review replicates this by avoiding the real-time directness that allows people to course-correct while it still matters.

**Solving problems rather than developing the person.** A manager who was never coached — just directed — directs rather than coaches. Problems get fixed faster in the short term. Engineers do not develop the judgment to fix them independently.

## Breaking the template

Breaking the inherited template requires examining it explicitly. What was useful in how you were managed? What cost you? What patterns are you replicating unconsciously?

The best engineering managers I have seen are the ones who can answer those questions. Not perfectly — nobody manages perfectly — but with enough self-awareness to interrupt the patterns they did not choose.
