The uncomfortable statistic that justifies the entire experimentation discipline: at the companies that measure honestly, **most product changes don't improve the metrics they target** — the published figures from mature experimentation cultures hover around one-third win, one-third neutral, one-third *harm*. Every organization that doesn't run controlled experiments is shipping that same distribution blind, celebrating the launches and attributing the metric drift to seasonality. A/B testing is not a growth hack; it's the only mechanism that reliably distinguishes "we shipped it and the metric moved" from "it moved the metric" — and building it trustworthily is an engineering discipline with sharp edges.

## The foundation: assignment you can bet the roadmap on

Everything downstream of assignment inherits its flaws. The requirements: **deterministic, sticky bucketing** (hash of unit ID and experiment salt — the same user sees the same variant across sessions and devices-where-identifiable, because a user oscillating between experiences is both a broken measurement and a broken product), **the right randomization unit** (users for UX changes; but tenant-level for B2B features where users within an account interfere, session-level only where memory-lessness is real, and cluster/geo-level where network effects contaminate — the interference question that most naive platforms never ask), **sample-ratio checking as an alarm, not an afterthought** (a 50/50 split arriving as 50.8/49.2 is not noise at scale — it's biased assignment or differential attrition, and it invalidates the experiment; the SRM check is the smoke detector of experimentation, cheap and non-negotiable), and **layered namespaces** for concurrent experiments (orthogonal hashing so overlapping tests don't correlate — the infrastructure that lets fifty teams experiment simultaneously without a coordination committee). This is also where the feature-flag estate earns its second job: the flag infrastructure *is* the assignment infrastructure, with the experiment layer adding stable bucketing and exposure logging — and exposure logging done right (log *who was actually exposed*, at the moment of exposure, not who was assigned) is the difference between measuring your feature and measuring your assignment table.

## The statistics: where confidence gets manufactured or earned

The failure modes are few, famous, and still everywhere: **peeking** (checking the dashboard daily and stopping when significance appears — a procedure that converts a 5% false-positive rate into a 30%+ one; the fixes are pre-committed durations and power calculations, or sequential-testing methods designed for continuous monitoring — most serious platforms now ship the latter), **the multiple-comparisons machine** (twenty metrics × ten segments = the guarantee of "significant" findings in noise; the fixes: **one pre-declared primary metric** per experiment, a small set of guardrail metrics — latency, errors, unsubscribes, the do-no-harm set — and everything else labeled exploratory, feeding hypotheses rather than decisions), **underpowered tests** (the two-week test that could only detect a 10% lift, run on a change whose realistic effect is 1% — it returns "no significant effect" that means nothing; power math before launch, and the organizational honesty that small effects need big samples or shouldn't be tested this way), and **novelty and primacy effects** (week-one behavior misrepresents steady state — durations that cover adaptation, and holdouts for the changes worth long-run truth). None of this requires a statistics department to *apply* — it requires a platform that defaults to it and refuses the corrupt shortcuts, which is precisely what the build-vs-buy evaluation should test: not the dashboard's beauty but whether the platform *lets* you peek-and-stop.

**Guardrails deserve their own emphasis:** every experiment monitors the small set of sacred metrics with automatic alerting (and shutdown authority for the severe cases) — because the true catastrophic outcome isn't a failed test, it's the conversion win that shipped with a latency regression nobody was watching. The vitals-and-error-budget thinking, applied to product change.

## The platform and the culture: which is the hard part?

The platform components are now well-commoditized (assignment SDKs, exposure pipelines, metric computation over the warehouse, sequential stats, SRM checks — the buy side has matured; the build case follows the usual framework and mostly loses below hyperscale). The hard part is cultural, and it's three specific habits:

**Pre-registration as ritual.** Hypothesis, primary metric, expected effect, duration, and decision rule — written *before* launch, referenced *at* the decision. This single artifact kills the post-hoc rationalization ("well, it improved this other metric...") that otherwise converts every experiment into a Rorschach test. The experiment review meeting reads the pre-registration first, the results second.

**Losing gracefully, in public.** An organization where a "failed" experiment damages the proposer's standing gets fewer experiments, gamed metrics, and quiet non-publication of inconvenient results — the file-drawer problem, in-house. The mature posture celebrates *informative* results regardless of direction (the harm you didn't ship is the platform's clearest ROI), maintains a searchable archive of all results (institutional memory that prevents the same losing idea shipping three reorgs later), and treats the two-thirds non-win rate as the expected base rate rather than a team failure.

**Experimenting on the right altitude.** The critique that experimentation optimizes local hills (button colors) while missing mountains is a critique of usage, not method: mature organizations run the small tests continuously *and* structure the big bets (pricing, redesigns, new surfaces) as holdout-measured rollouts with longer horizons — the same rigor at strategic altitude, where the cost of self-deception is highest.

The summary for the platform review: deterministic sticky assignment with SRM alarms; exposure-logged, layered experiments riding the flag infrastructure; one primary metric pre-declared, guardrails automated, sequential stats or committed durations — no peeking; power math before launch; results archived, losses celebrated, decisions read against pre-registrations. Built this way, experimentation becomes what it's actually for — not a conversion-optimization gadget, but the organization's epistemology: the standing answer to "how do we know that's true?" for every claim about what users want. Most companies have opinions about their product. The experimenting ones have evidence — including, humblingly, about themselves.
