The specialist-generalist tension in engineering careers is often framed as a choice between depth and breadth, as though one person cannot have both. The most useful senior technical people I have worked with have genuine depth in at least two or three domains and broad enough exposure to reason credibly about the rest. The generalist framing undersells what they actually bring.

The function of the senior technical generalist in a complex organisation: they are the people who recognise that the latency problem the networking team is investigating is caused by a data access pattern the database team introduced, which was triggered by a product change the application team shipped without a corresponding infrastructure review. None of the specialists can see this because each is working within their domain's visibility. The generalist sees the interaction.

This function requires going deep enough in multiple domains to understand where the seams are. Someone who knows distributed systems well enough to design message queue architectures and knows databases well enough to understand replication lag and knows application architecture well enough to trace a call graph can diagnose cross-domain problems that pure specialists cannot. That combination of depth is not shallow breadth — it is a different and more demanding accumulation.

The career development implication: engineers who deliberately expand their depth into adjacent domains become more valuable as systems grow in complexity, not less. The path requires resisting the specialisation pressure long enough to develop genuine competence in a second or third domain. The short-term cost is being less maximally expert in the first domain. The long-term value is the ability to see the full system.
