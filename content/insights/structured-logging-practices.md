Logging is the telemetry everyone has and almost nobody designs. Metrics get dashboards reviews and traces get instrumentation sprints, while logs just… accumulate — printf archaeology wrapped in an ingestion bill that grows 40% annually until finance asks why the observability vendor costs more than the compute. The waste and the weakness have the same root: logs treated as *text a developer emits* rather than what production logging actually is — **a stream of structured events, designed for machines to filter and humans to trust, with an explicit cost model.** Here's the design.

## What to measure

**Field completeness and cost per debugged incident.** If 940 of 1,000 eligible log records have a usable trace identifier, correlation coverage is 94%. Track ingestion volume and inspect sensitive-field redaction. Better coverage is valuable when operators can reconstruct incidents faster.

## Events, not sentences

The foundational shift: a log line is a **JSON event with a schema**, not a sentence with variables interpolated into it. `"Payment failed for order 12345 after 3 retries"` is a sentence — grep-able if you remember the phrasing, aggregatable never. The event version carries `event: payment_failed`, `order_id`, `retry_count`, `failure_reason`, `duration_ms` — and now "failure rate by reason over time" is a query instead of a regex safari.

The schema disciplines that make event streams queryable at estate scale: **a small set of standard fields on every event** (timestamp, severity, service, version, environment, plus the correlation set below) — adopt an existing convention (OpenTelemetry's semantic conventions or the ECS-class schemas) rather than inventing one, because the payoff of shared field names compounds across every service and every dashboard; **stable event names** treated like API surface (renaming `payment_failed` breaks every alert and saved query downstream — version and deprecate, don't mutate); **consistent types per field** (an `order_id` that's a string here and a number there breaks indexing in ways discovered at query time); and **one event per logical occurrence**, not a narrative of five lines that must be mentally reassembled — the request-summary event (one rich line per request with outcome, duration, and key attributes — the classic "canonical log line" pattern) is worth more than fifty step-by-step murmurs, and costs less.

## Context is what makes logs debuggable

A log line's value is mostly its *joinability*. The non-negotiable correlation set: **trace and request IDs** (propagated via OpenTelemetry context — the integration that transforms logs from standalone artifacts into the detail layer of distributed traces: find the slow trace, jump to its logs, done), **principal context** (tenant/user identifiers — pseudonymous where privacy demands), and **causal context** where it exists (job ID, message ID, saga/workflow ID — the threads that let you follow one business transaction across services and hours). Inject these once at the boundary (middleware, contextvars/MDC — the async-safe variants covered in the respective stack discussions) so every log line in the request's lifetime carries them without per-callsite effort. A codebase where engineers manually remember to include the request ID is a codebase where a third of the lines don't have it — and that third is always where the incident is.

The counterpart discipline is **what must never be in logs**: secrets, tokens, passwords (redaction at the logging-library layer, plus scanners in CI and on the stream — because one debug line logging full request headers is a credential leak with retention), and unminimized personal data (logs are the compliance surface nobody maps — data-protection rules apply to them fully, deletion requests included, and "it's just logs" is not a defense anyone enjoys presenting).

## Levels, sampling, and the cost model

Level discipline, stripped of folklore: **ERROR** means something requiring action failed (and should be alarm-worthy in aggregate — an ERROR that's routine is mislabeled); **WARN** means degraded-but-handled (retries that succeeded, fallbacks taken — the early-warning band); **INFO** carries the business-and-request events that tell the system's story (the canonical lines); **DEBUG** exists for humans actively investigating and is *off* in production by default — with the crucial modern amendment that it should be **dynamically enable-able** (per service, per tenant, per request-percentage, at runtime, without deploys) because "redeploy with debug logging" is a 45-minute incident tax that runtime toggles reduce to seconds.

Then the economics, because logging is priced per byte and emitted per request: **not every event deserves 100% capture.** The sane defaults — keep all ERROR/WARN, keep 100% of request summaries for errored/slow requests, *sample* the happy-path INFO stream (1–10% preserves statistical visibility at a tenth of the bill), always-keep anything tied to a sampled trace (consistency with your tracing decisions, or the join breaks), and let high-volume DEBUG-adjacent events earn their retention explicitly. Add **tiered retention** — hot searchable days, warm queryable weeks, cold object-storage archive for the compliance horizon — and the bill drops from "growth-limiting" to "proportionate," which is the actual goal: the organizations that log everything forever at full index eventually respond by logging *nothing* useful, and the pendulum's both ends lose.

The operating loop that keeps it healthy: a monthly look at ingestion by service and event (the top ten events are reliably 80% of volume, and half of them serve no query anyone has run — delete or sample them), alerts on log-volume anomalies (a 10× spike is either an incident or an accidental debug-loop, both worth knowing), and the standing question in code review — *who queries this line, and what decision does it inform?* — which is the logging version of the test-value question, and prunes with the same efficiency.

## The summary schema

Structured events against a shared convention; stable names treated as API; one rich canonical line per request; correlation IDs injected at boundaries and present everywhere; secrets and PII engineered out at the library layer; levels that mean things, with DEBUG dynamically switchable; sampling and tiered retention as designed policy rather than vendor-bill archaeology; and a review loop that deletes what nobody queries. None of it is glamorous, all of it compounds — because logs are where every incident investigation actually ends up at 3 AM, and the difference between fifteen minutes and three hours of that investigation was decided months earlier, by whether someone designed the events or just let the printfs accumulate.

## Write one event specification that producers and consumers can test

Start with an event that already drives a support workflow or alert. Define its name, the logical occurrence it represents, which service owns it, and when it is emitted. For an illustrative delivery status workflow, choose an event meaning that a durable status transition committed. Emitting the event before commit records an intention as an outcome. Emitting it from each layer that handles the same update produces several apparent outcomes per operation. Choose the point that can observe the agreed effect and make that choice visible in the schema contract.

Annotate each field with its type, units, cardinality expectations, business meaning, and access requirements. Suppose the event carries outcome, change origin, duration in milliseconds, occurrence time, observation time, a trace identifier when available, and an opaque operation reference. Deployment version and service identity may be resource fields supplied by the telemetry configuration. Supplying the event body and resource fields through separate channels requires a mapping test. Renaming a field in one export format cannot silently change the contract for queries written against the other.

Test schema compatibility against realistic readers. Have old and new producers emit through the same pipeline and verify that both are parsed. Replay saved alert queries, search filters, and archive ingestion. Adding a field can still break a strict consumer or a dynamic index if that field alternates between a string and an object. Establish the difference between an absent field, a field that was explicitly removed by policy, and a missing value because validation failed. When an event transitions to a new schema version, record migration progress by the number of compatible readers rather than by the number of services deployed.

## Treat timestamps and correlation as data with quality thresholds

Record the time an event occurred separately from the time the collection pipeline observed it when both are available. The OpenTelemetry logging model defines both concepts. This distinction helps diagnose a backlogged log shipment: the operation can be old even while the collector is healthy enough to accept a new batch. Use an agreed UTC representation and preserve timezone context if an external event has a local timestamp. Record known clock uncertainty. A small negative difference between timestamps is a clock or transformation signal, not negative processing time.

Test correlation on an asynchronous operation that begins as an HTTP request and ends in a worker. Start it without a debug session. Verify that the initiating service records the request, the queue producer identifies the outgoing work item, and the consumer logs an outcome with the expected relationship to upstream context. A retried message or long-running workflow may need its own operation identifier rather than the same request identifier forever. If propagation creates links instead of a shared trace, confirm that investigators can navigate from the recorded operation to both sides.

Set coverage targets on clearly defined populations. In a hypothetical sample of 2,000 request-completion events, 1,880 carry valid trace identifiers. That is 94% correlation coverage for those events. If a dashboard also finds 1,200 events with a trace actually present in the tracing backend, that is 60% navigability over the same population, assuming every backend match is accurate. Traces can be dropped or expire despite a valid identifier. Both statistics are useful and answer different questions. Break down missing correlation by route and sampling policy so normal background jobs cannot dilute an HTTP instrumentation change.

## Run a retention and access exercise that includes redaction failures

Decide which fields may be present before collection. Remove secrets, authorization tokens, and unnecessary request content where the event is constructed. Add a second review at collection or export to catch incompatible producers. A pipeline can fail while protective rules are pending or restart with unexpected configuration. Simulate an event with a harmless synthetic marker that represents an excluded field and check the primary sink, dead-letter storage, archives, console logs, and error reports. A report that tests only the query backend gives an incomplete account of exposure. Keep marker fixtures separate from real customer data.

Choose durable identifiers for debugging carefully. An unpredictable service-generated request reference is generally easier to share safely than a direct account identifier. Hashing a predictable field does not automatically make it anonymous. Join restricted identifiers in a deliberately controlled system if the workflow requires them. Test that an ordinary support account can retrieve the event evidence it needs without broad access to restricted attributes. Verify that rate limits and redaction do not leave multiple support teams copying raw events into informal channels.

Measure recoverability by restoring a small time range from the archive. Verify that the old schema and metadata remain readable, the restore does not exceed the expected workflow window, and access grants are scoped to the restored range. If a required field cannot be whitelisted for privacy reasons, test a safer investigative route. Establish concretely whether data ages out of all sinks, including backup, restore, and secondary routing destinations. Native platform retention may have distinct behavior across those sinks.

## Reconcile the volume estimate against observed pipeline work

A worked budget can guide the experiment. Suppose completion events are emitted at 500 per second and average 800 bytes before compression. That is 400,000 bytes per second, approximately 34.56 decimal GB over 24 hours at constant traffic. Removing an unused repeated field that saves 120 bytes reduces the rate by 60,000 bytes per second, or 5.184 decimal GB per day. The billing impact depends on the provider's billing boundary, accepted record counts, enrichment, compression, and retention. Check bytes at the exporter and provider rather than applying the estimated percentage to an entire observability bill.

Tie the change to an investigation task. Ask two teams to reconstruct the sequence of states in the same cluster of sample issues without knowing whether they are using the prior or revised pipeline. Record elapsed time, outcomes they could verify, and gaps that caused them to stop. An event that becomes smaller but no longer identifies the affected trajectory can cost more in investigation time than it saves in ingestion. Rehearse a dependency failure that creates a burst of repeated errors and check the exporter queue, the integrity of exceptional events, and how the platform distinguishes intentional suppression from delivery loss.
