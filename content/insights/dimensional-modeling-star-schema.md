Every few years a new platform arrives with the same implicit promise — columnar engines, then big data, then the lakehouse: *storage is cheap and compute is elastic now, so you can skip the modeling*. And every few years, organizations that believed it rediscover the same swamp: a thousand bespoke tables nobody can join, seventeen definitions of revenue, dashboards that disagree, and analysts spending most of their time re-deriving what a modeled layer would have made a lookup. Dimensional modeling — the Kimball-lineage discipline of facts and dimensions — keeps outliving its obituaries for a reason the platform pitches miss: **it was never a storage optimization; it's an interface design for humans asking questions of data**, and humans haven't changed.

## The core: facts, dimensions, and the grain declaration

The model's two citizens: **fact tables** hold measurements of business events — one row per occurrence at a declared **grain** (per order line, per payment, per sensor reading) — with numeric measures and foreign keys outward; **dimension tables** hold the descriptive context — who, what, where, which product, which day — denormalized into wide, human-readable tables that queries filter and group by. The star shape (facts center, dimensions radiating) gives the analytical engine exactly what it optimizes for and gives the analyst something better: *a predictable question grammar* — every business question becomes "measures from the fact, sliced by attributes of the dimensions," which is why BI tools, semantic layers, and — increasingly load-bearing — **AI query generation** all work dramatically better against dimensional models than against operational-schema sprawl (a star schema is, in context-engineering terms, a machine-legible statement of what the business measures; text-to-SQL against 400 normalized OLTP tables hallucinates, against a documented star it mostly just works).

The single most valuable discipline in the practice costs one sentence per table: **declare the grain, explicitly, first** — "one row per order line item as of latest state" — because every modeling error worth an incident traces to grain confusion: mixed grains in one table (order-level discounts jammed into line-level rows — the double-counting machine), or measures summed across a grain they don't own (the classic dashboard-inflation bug). Grain declared, everything else follows: which measures are additive (summable across all dimensions — the comfortable case), semi-additive (balances: summable across accounts, *not* across time — the snapshot-fact case), or non-additive (ratios and percentages — computed from additive components at query time, never stored pre-divided, which is the "average of averages" lie in table form).

## Slowly changing dimensions: history as a design decision

The famous SCD taxonomy is really one question — *when a customer moves segments, what should last year's revenue report say?* — with two honest answers: **Type 1** (overwrite — history reflects current attributes; simpler, and correct when the business genuinely wants "as of now" reporting) and **Type 2** (versioned rows with effective-date ranges and a current flag — history reflects *what was true at the time*, which is what finance, compliance, and any honest trend analysis require). The engineering notes that spare pain: Type 2 demands **surrogate keys** (the fact row points at the *version* of the customer that existed at event time — natural keys can't express this, which is the actual argument for surrogates, not warehouse folklore), the current-flag-plus-date-range pattern needs the same gap-and-overlap testing as any interval data (the data-quality checks that catch the customer with two "current" rows), and the CDC pipelines from integration practice are the natural feed (change events *are* SCD deltas — the pattern compositions keep converging). The pragmatic default: Type 2 for the dimensions whose history the business will ever ask about (customer, product, org hierarchy), Type 1 for correction-shaped attributes, and the decision *documented per column*, because "did we mean to lose that history?" is a question with no good retroactive answer.

## The modern amendments: what actually changed

Honest scorekeeping on the platform era's claims: **normalization for storage is dead** (columnar compression made wide denormalized dimensions nearly free — the snowflake-vs-star debate resolved decisively toward star; the compression-and-encoding economics reward exactly the repetitive columns dimensions have), **One Big Table has a real niche** (pre-joined wide tables as *serving* artifacts — performance-optimized projections for specific dashboards or feature stores, generated *from* the dimensional layer; the mistake is OBT as the *modeling* layer, where every new question re-litigates the joins that dimensions had already settled, and every definition fork breeds — the derived-store discipline applies: OBTs are caches of the model, rebuildable, never the truth), **the semantic layer is dimensional modeling's new enforcement point** (metrics defined once — measure, grain, allowed dimensions — and served to every tool; the seventeen-revenues problem is a governance failure the metrics layer exists to close, and it works precisely because it's a dimensional contract wearing modern tooling), and **the transformation stack changed the workflow, not the design** (dbt-style layered builds — staging → dimensional core → serving marts — with the model under version control, tested (grain uniqueness, referential integrity, SCD validity as CI checks per the data-contract disciplines), and documented where analysts and AI agents can read it; the medallion-architecture vocabulary maps cleanly: the gold layer *is* the dimensional model, or it's the swamp with better branding).

The placement judgment, because the discipline has boundaries: dimensional modeling serves *human-and-BI analytical consumption* — it is not the shape for ML feature pipelines (feature stores have their own grain discipline), streaming analytics (the stream-processing models), or operational queries (the OLTP normalization rules stand). The estates that thrive run the layered flow: raw → cleaned → *dimensional core as the semantic contract* → purpose-shaped serving projections — with the middle layer as the only place "what does revenue mean" gets decided.

The closing argument mirrors every interface discipline in this series: cheap storage and elastic compute made it *possible* to skip modeling, and simultaneously made the cost of skipping it larger — because the bottleneck was never the terabytes; it was the humans (and now the models) trying to ask questions and trust answers. Facts, dimensions, declared grains, and versioned history are how a data platform states its meaning once, legibly, for every consumer that follows. Platforms keep changing; the questions — and the questioners — keep needing the interface.
