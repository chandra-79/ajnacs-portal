The GPU fleet is where every FinOps and capacity discipline in this series meets its most expensive test case: accelerators priced at multiples of equivalent CPU spend, supply constrained enough that capacity is *reserved ahead* rather than summoned on demand, and — the finding that recurs in every honest internal audit — **utilization numbers that would trigger an intervention anywhere else in the estate**. Published and anecdotal figures for real ML platforms routinely land in the 20–40% range for training fleets and worse for inference off-peak, which at GPU prices is not a rounding error; it's frequently the largest single waste line in a modern engineering budget, hiding behind the assumption that AI infrastructure is supposed to be expensive.

## The measurement honesty problem

GPU utilization lies in layers, and the first discipline is knowing which layer you're reading: **allocation utilization** (the pod/instance holds the GPU — what cluster dashboards show) versus **kernel utilization** (something is executing — what `nvidia-smi`-style metrics show) versus **effective throughput** (the arithmetic units doing model-shaped work — what profiler-level metrics like SM efficiency and memory-bandwidth saturation approximate). The gaps between layers are where the money goes: the notebook holding four GPUs while its owner is at lunch (allocated, idle — the workstation-reservation pattern that quietly dominates research clusters), the training job blocked on data loading (allocated, kernel-idle — the input-pipeline bottleneck that GPU metrics *can't* see and profilers reveal: the storage-and-preprocessing tier from data-pipeline practice failing to feed the accelerator), and the inference server running kernels at 15% occupancy (busy-looking, throughput-poor — the batching economics from inference practice, unapplied). The instrumentation floor: per-GPU, per-team, per-job metrics on all three layers, on the same FinOps dashboards as spend — because "our GPUs are all allocated" and "our GPUs are working" differ by the price of a small engineering org.

## The toolkit: sharing, scheduling, and the two workload shapes

The remedies sort by the two GPU workload shapes, which want opposite things. **Training/batch** (throughput-oriented, preemption-tolerant in principle): the scheduling tier does the heavy lifting — gang scheduling for multi-GPU jobs (the all-or-nothing allocation that prevents deadlocked partial claims), **queue-based platforms over hand-reservation** (the Kueue/Volcano-class batch layer on Kubernetes, or the managed equivalents — jobs declare shapes and priorities, the scheduler packs the fleet; the difference between a reservation culture and a queue culture is routinely 20+ points of utilization, because humans hoard and queues don't), **preemptible tiers with checkpointing discipline** (the training job that checkpoints per the batch-resumability rules can ride lower-priority capacity and absorb eviction — which unlocks both spot economics and the priority ladder: production retraining preempts research exploration, per the criticality-tier logic), and the **input-pipeline engineering** that keeps the expensive silicon fed (prefetching, parallel decode, cached datasets — profiling says an embarrassing share of "GPU shortage" is actually data starvation).

**Inference/serving** (latency-oriented, always-on): the utilization levers are the serving-stack disciplines — continuous batching and KV-cache management as the foundation, **fractional sharing for the small-model tier** (MIG-style hardware partitioning for isolation-grade splits, time-slicing for the tolerant tier — the one-model-one-GPU default wastes most of a modern accelerator on anything below flagship scale), model routing so small requests hit small models on shared slices (the routing economics, again the biggest lever), and autoscaling with the honest cold-start math (model-loading minutes mean scale-up leads demand — the capacity-headroom trade, priced at GPU rates; scale-to-zero only where the latency contract allows).

Underneath both: the **capacity portfolio** discipline from cloud-economics practice, sharpened by scarcity — committed/reserved capacity for the modeled floor, on-demand and spot for the crest, and the *utilization-versus-commitment* review run quarterly, because idle reserved GPUs are the purest waste in the building, and over-commitment is how they happen.

## The organizational layer: allocation is where the number moves

The technical toolkit stalls without the incentive layer, and GPU estates reproduce every commons failure: hoarding (the team that fought for quota and won't release it — allocation-as-property), invisible cost (research clusters where nobody sees a price), and the squeaky-wheel capacity process (allocation by escalation rather than by return). The countermeasures are familiar economics applied firmly: **showback/chargeback at the team-and-job grain** (the tagging-and-attribution discipline — a GPU-hour has a price and a consumer, visible weekly), **quota with reclamation** (idle allocations above threshold auto-release to the queue — the lease model over the property model, with the utilization telemetry as enforcement), **a real priority ladder adjudicated once** (the criticality-inventory review: production inference > scheduled retraining > time-boxed research bursts > exploratory — decided calmly, enforced by the scheduler, revisited quarterly — not re-litigated per Slack thread), and **the efficiency review for the top consumers** (the ten biggest jobs get profiling attention — the total-time discipline from performance practice; a 30% throughput win on the flagship training job is a hardware tranche nobody has to buy). And the meta-metric that keeps leadership honest: **cost per unit of ML outcome** (per training run to target quality, per thousand inferences at SLO — the unit economics that connect the fleet to the business), because "we need more GPUs" is only evaluable against "at what utilization, producing what."

The closing calibration: GPU scarcity gets narrated as a procurement problem, and for most organizations it's substantially an *engineering and governance* problem wearing procurement's costume — the fleet you have, measured honestly, queued rather than hoarded, fed properly, shared fractionally, and allocated by priority, is routinely half again larger than it appears. The most expensive computer in the building deserves the estate's best operational disciplines; mostly it gets its newest. Closing that gap is the cheapest capacity anyone will buy this year.
