Enterprise identity has a famous half and a forgotten half. The famous half is authentication — SSO, OIDC, the login box — well-understood and well-covered. The forgotten half is **lifecycle**: how accounts come to exist in each of a company's hundreds of applications, how their permissions track role changes, and — the half of the half that fails audits — how they *cease to exist* when someone leaves. Every access review that finds the contractor from 2023 still holding admin in the CRM, every offboarding checklist with forty manual steps, every "mover" who accumulated three roles' worth of permissions across a decade — that's the provisioning layer, absent or improvised. The joiner-mover-leaver problem is the most consequential boring problem in enterprise security, and SCIM is the standard that industrialized it.

## The problem shape: JML and the accumulation disease

**Joiners** need accounts and baseline access on day one (the onboarding that takes three weeks of ticket-chasing is a provisioning failure wearing an IT-process costume). **Movers** are the subtle case: role changes should *re-derive* access, but manual processes only ever *add* — nobody files the ticket to remove the old team's permissions — so long-tenured employees accumulate access monotonically (the least-privilege erosion from IAM practice, at the human layer; the access-analysis finding is that permission sets correlate with tenure, not role, in almost every unautomated estate). **Leavers** are the audit case and the breach case: departure must revoke everything, promptly, across every system — and "every system" is the hard part, because the SaaS estate sprawls (the shadow-IT census from DMARC and CT-log monitoring applies here too: you can't deprovision from the app you don't know about).

The architecture that solves it is **directory-as-source-of-truth with automated propagation**: HR events (hire, transfer, terminate) flow into the identity provider; group/role membership derives from attributes (department, title, cost center — *policy computes access*, per the role-catalog disciplines); and provisioning connectors push the consequences into every downstream application. The leaver flow is the one to engineer hardest: termination in HR should cascade to session revocation (the stateful-session architecture earning its keep — this is why revocable sessions at the edge matter), token invalidation, and account deactivation across the estate within *minutes to hours*, measured — because the deprovisioning SLA is the metric auditors ask for and attackers race.

## SCIM: the protocol, and its sharp edges

**SCIM 2.0** is the standard that makes propagation interoperable: a RESTful API (Users and Groups resources, standardized schemas with extension points) that identity providers call against applications — create user, update attributes, deactivate, manage group membership — replacing the per-app CSV imports and bespoke connectors of the dark ages. The mechanics matter to both sides: operations are the usual CRUD plus **PATCH** (whose SCIM dialect — path expressions, multi-valued attribute operations — is where most implementation bugs live), filtering (`userName eq "..."`) for reconciliation queries, and the **externalId** linkage that keeps the IdP's identity and the app's identity joined through renames and email changes (the stable-identifier discipline from API design: email is a display attribute, not a key — every SCIM integration that keyed on email meets the marriage-rename bug eventually).

The operational truths practitioners learn: **SCIM is eventually consistent with silent failure modes** — connectors retry, but a failing integration quietly accumulates drift, so **reconciliation is mandatory** (periodic full-sync comparison between IdP truth and app state, alerting on deltas — the webhook-reconciliation pattern verbatim: push for speed, poll for truth); **deactivate versus delete is a policy decision per app** (deactivation preserves audit trails and re-hire continuity; deletion satisfies data-minimization — the privacy-engineering retention rules deciding per data class); and **groups are where the model strains** — SCIM moves group *membership* well, but mapping IdP groups to app-internal roles is per-app configuration that drifts (the entitlement-mapping table is governance surface, reviewed like the IAM role catalog it mirrors).

## Building the app side: SCIM as a product requirement

For anyone *building* B2B software, provisioning support graduated from enterprise-tier afterthought to deal requirement (it sits alongside SSO in every security questionnaire, and its absence is a procurement blocker at serious buyers). The implementation checklist beyond "expose the endpoints": **treat the SCIM API as tier-one** (it's an unattended write path into your authorization model — the API-security disciplines apply doubly: token-scoped per tenant, rate-limited, audit-logged per operation), **implement deactivation semantics correctly** (active=false must kill sessions and tokens *now* — the customer's leaver SLA is only as good as your revocation path; a SCIM deactivation that leaves live sessions until natural expiry is a compliance finding you've shipped to every customer), **support the reconciliation queries** honestly (filtered listing at scale — the pagination disciplines — because the IdP's full-sync is your API's load test), and **map roles explicitly** (documented group-to-role semantics, not heuristics — the customer's auditor will trace one user's access end-to-end, and "it depends on group name patterns" is not an answer that closes deals).

## The program view: identity lifecycle as measured infrastructure

The maturity ladder mirrors every governance discipline in this series: from *manual tickets* (unmeasured, accumulating) → *SSO plus manual provisioning* (authentication solved, lifecycle not — the common resting state, and a false summit) → *automated JML from HR-driven directory truth with SCIM propagation* → *the closed loop*: deprovisioning SLAs on dashboards, quarterly access reviews computed from actual grants (not spreadsheets — the access-analyzer pattern applied to SaaS), mover re-derivation enforced ("access follows role" as a reconciliation rule, not an aspiration), and the app-inventory census wired to discovery (expense-system and SSO-log mining for the SaaS nobody registered). The metrics that make it real: time-to-productive-access for joiners, time-to-full-revocation for leavers, drift findings per reconciliation cycle, and orphaned-account count trending to zero.

The strategic framing for the security review: authentication got the industry's attention because it's where users *feel* identity — but lifecycle is where identity *risk* actually lives, in the gap between "who should have access" and "who does," integrated over every departure, transfer, and forgotten app in the estate's history. SCIM and the JML machinery are how that gap gets an SLA — and the estates that treat provisioning as infrastructure rather than ticket queue are the ones whose access reviews are confirmations instead of discoveries.
