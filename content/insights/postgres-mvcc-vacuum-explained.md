Postgres operational knowledge has a peculiar shape: a pile of seemingly unrelated folklore — "watch out for bloat," "tune autovacuum on busy tables," "beware transaction-ID wraparound," "long transactions are bad," "updates are expensive" — that turns out to be one fact wearing five costumes. The fact: **Postgres implements multi-version concurrency control by keeping old row versions in the table itself** — an UPDATE writes a *new* row version alongside the old one; a DELETE marks the row dead without removing it; and readers see whichever version their snapshot entitles them to (the isolation-level machinery, implemented as version visibility). The design buys the crown jewel — readers never block writers, writers never block readers — and it creates a maintenance debt: **dead versions accumulate until something reclaims them**, and that something is VACUUM. Understand this one trade and the folklore becomes mechanism.

## The consequences, one costume at a time

**Bloat** is the debt made visible: update-heavy tables carry dead versions inline — the table (and its indexes) grow beyond live-data size, scans read pages full of corpses, and the cache-hit economics degrade (the working-set math, taxed by ghosts). **Update cost** follows: every UPDATE is physically an insert-plus-tombstone, and — the detail with design consequences — *every index on the table needs a new entry for the new version* (the index-count write-amplification from indexing practice, with MVCC as the multiplier), except when the **HOT (heap-only tuple) optimization** applies: updates that touch no indexed column and fit on the same page skip the index maintenance entirely — which is the mechanical reason behind two schema-design rules: don't index columns you update constantly, and leave per-page slack (`fillfactor` below 100) on update-hot tables so HOT chains have room. **Long-running transactions** get their bad reputation here too: VACUUM can only reclaim versions *older than the oldest snapshot still alive* — one analyst's forgotten idle-in-transaction session (or one abandoned replication slot, per the CDC vigilance) holds the reclamation horizon back for the whole database, and bloat accumulates fleet-wide while everyone blames the workload; the countermeasures are policy, not tuning (idle-in-transaction timeouts, slot monitoring, and the batch-job discipline of short transactions).

**And the famous one — transaction-ID wraparound**: row versions are stamped with 32-bit transaction IDs, whose comparison arithmetic requires old stamps to be periodically "frozen" (marked forever-visible) before the counter wraps — VACUUM's second job. Defer it long enough (autovacuum disabled "for performance," or a wraparound-horizon held back at billions of transactions) and Postgres protects data integrity the only way it can: refusing new writes until a freeze completes — the legendary outage genre where the database "just stopped." The monitoring that makes wraparound a non-event is one query (database and table age versus the autovacuum-freeze threshold, alarmed with generous headroom) — it belongs on every Postgres estate's dashboard, and its absence is the only reason the incident genre still exists.

## Autovacuum: tuning the janitor for the building it actually cleans

Autovacuum's defaults are sized for modest tables and betray the big busy ones: the scale-factor model (vacuum when dead tuples exceed ~20% of the table) means a billion-row table accumulates two hundred million corpses before cleanup begins — far too late. The tuning that works is *per-table and threshold-shaped*: on the hot tables, drop the scale factor toward flat thresholds (vacuum after N-thousand dead tuples, not after a percentage), raise autovacuum's cost limits so it can actually keep pace (the default throttling made sense on 2005 disks; on NVMe it's a janitor on a coffee break — the disk-headroom trade from capacity practice, spent on maintenance), and add workers where many tables compete. The complementary statistics job (ANALYZE, riding the same machinery) feeds the query planner — the stale-statistics plan disasters from plan-reading practice are frequently autovacuum starvation wearing an optimizer costume. And the honest escalation path: bloat already accumulated doesn't shrink via VACUUM (it reclaims space for *reuse*, not for return to the OS) — the repack tier (`pg_repack`-class online rebuilds) is the remediation for the table that got away, scheduled like the maintenance it is.

**Monitoring closes the loop** with a short vital-sign list: dead-tuple counts and bloat estimates per table (trend, not snapshot), last-autovacuum timestamps on the hot tables (the table that hasn't been visited in days *is* the incident precursor), the oldest-transaction/slot horizon (the reclamation blocker detector), and wraparound age (above). Four queries, one dashboard, and the entire folklore genre — the mystery bloat, the sudden slowness, the write freeze — becomes a set of alarms that fire days before users notice anything.

The closing calibration: MVCC-in-heap is neither flaw nor accident — it's a deliberate trade (concurrent reads at the cost of background maintenance) that Postgres made differently than its peers (the undo-log designs relocate the debt rather than deleting it — every MVCC database pays somewhere), and operating Postgres well is mostly *respecting the trade*: schemas that let HOT work, transactions that end, autovacuum tuned to the workload's actual churn, and the four vital signs watched. The database's most mystifying behaviors are, underneath, one honest mechanism keeping one honest promise — readers and writers, never waiting on each other — and sending the bill to whoever reads the maintenance manual. Be the estate where someone has.
