There's a moment in good design reviews that separates engineering from opinion: someone does thirty seconds of arithmetic and an entire architectural option quietly dies. "That's 40 billion writes a month — at DynamoDB pricing that's six figures annually; next option." "Each request fans out to 30 services at 2ms each — sequentially that's your whole latency budget; this needs parallelism or fewer hops." Back-of-envelope calculation is the cheapest analysis in the field — no prototypes, no benchmarks, no meetings — and it's systematically undertaught, treated as interview theater rather than what it is: the daily filter that keeps designs tethered to physics and prices before anyone writes code.

## What to measure

**Average and peak throughput.** Five million requests a day average about 58 requests/s. An assumed 10× peak produces about 580 requests/s for a first sizing pass. Replace that multiplier with observed peak windows once traffic data exists.

*Illustrative measurement. Any numbers in this example are hypothetical.*

## The numbers worth carrying

The method needs a small mental almanac — order-of-magnitude values, not precision: **latency's ladder** (L1 cache ~1ns; RAM ~100ns; NVMe read ~100μs; same-zone network round trip ~0.5ms; cross-region ~50–150ms; disk-vs-memory is three orders of magnitude, and *cross-region-vs-same-zone is two* — the number underneath every data-locality and multi-region argument), **throughput's anchors** (a modern server pushes millions of simple ops/sec in memory, tens-of-thousands of database transactions/sec; a single Postgres comfortably serves thousands of mixed QPS before architecture is the answer; NICs are 10–100Gbps and rarely the bottleneck you think), **time's conversions** (1 day ≈ 86,400s — so 1M requests/day ≈ 12/sec, a number that has ended a thousand premature-scaling conversations; 1 month ≈ 2.6M seconds; a year of 1/sec ≈ 31M events), **storage's scale** (1KB per event × 1B events = 1TB — the "can this fit in one Postgres" arithmetic; RAM-per-node in the hundreds-of-GB means "just cache it all" is often literal), and **cost's handles** (object storage ~$0.02/GB-month; the egress multiple; a vCPU-hour's rough price; the 10–100× spread between hot-database and cold-object storage per GB — the tiering economics in one ratio). Precision is the enemy here: these move yearly, but their *ratios* — the orders of magnitude between tiers — are the stable furniture of judgment.

## The moves: a repeatable sequence

Real estimation is four moves run in order, each a one-liner:

**1. Traffic, decomposed.** DAU × actions/user/day ÷ 86,400 = average RPS; multiply by the peak factor (2–5× for consumer diurnal patterns, more for event-driven spikes — the burst-shape honesty from capacity practice); split reads from writes (the ratio drives everything downstream — a 100:1 read-heavy system is a caching problem, a write-heavy one is a partitioning problem).

**2. Data, integrated over time.** Payload size × write rate × retention = storage; growth rate versus the single-node comfort zone tells you *when* sharding becomes real rather than whether it's fashionable (the "we'll need it in year four" answer that reprioritizes a roadmap). Don't forget the multipliers: replication factor, index overhead, the derived copies every projection pattern creates.

**3. Bandwidth and fan-out.** Response size × RPS = egress (and its bill); per-request internal fan-out × hop latency, sequential versus parallel, against the latency budget (the arithmetic that turns "microservices" from an org-chart preference into a numbers conversation — the six-hop-99.9% composite-availability math belongs here too: six sequential 99.9% dependencies ≈ 99.4%, a number that redesigns diagrams).

**4. Dollars, always.** Every resource estimate gets a unit price and an annual figure — because "technically feasible" and "economically sane" diverge exactly where envelope math is cheapest to run (the build-vs-buy and serving-cost disciplines all begin as this move), and because a cost-per-request estimate is the number that lets product and engineering argue productively.

Then the two hygiene habits that make it engineering rather than numerology: **write the assumptions down** (the envelope's value is auditable reasoning — "assumed 2KB events, 90-day retention, 3× replication" is checkable and updatable; a bare conclusion is neither), and **bracket instead of point-guessing** (run the pessimistic and optimistic cases; if both land on the same side of the decision, the decision is robust to your ignorance — the calibrated-range discipline from estimation practice, applied to systems).

## Where it earns its keep

The applications beyond the design review: **sanity-checking vendor claims and benchmarks** (does the promised throughput exceed what the instance's memory bandwidth could physically deliver? — marketing decks fail envelope math at an instructive rate), **incident triage** ("the queue is growing at 5k/sec and drains at 3k/sec — we have 40 minutes of buffer" is envelope math under adrenaline, and the responders who do it steer better), **capacity and headroom reasoning** (the time-to-wall trajectory from capacity planning is an envelope calculation maintained quarterly), **prioritization** ("this optimization saves 2ms on a path that runs 100 times/day" versus "0.2ms on one that runs 10k/sec" — total-time thinking, the profiler's lesson, computable before profiling), and — increasingly — **AI-era feasibility** (tokens × price × volume is the new storage-math; many an LLM feature has died or been re-scoped in one line of per-request cost arithmetic, exactly as it should).

The deeper case for the habit is cultural: teams that estimate publicly develop a shared sense of *scale* — the collective instinct for what's big, what's cheap, what's absurd — which is the actual foundation under every judgment this series describes: choosing databases by working set, deployment strategies by blast radius, architectures by fan-out, tools by workload shape. None of those judgments require precision; all of them require knowing the orders of magnitude, and orders of magnitude are exactly what the envelope delivers. Thirty seconds of arithmetic before every consequential choice — assumptions written, ranges bracketed, dollars attached — is the highest-return ritual available to a technical organization. The prototype confirms; the envelope *decides*.

## Trace the work that one request creates

An arrival count is only the first row in an estimate. Convert arrivals into work at each bottleneck separately. A product search request might generate a routing decision, two reads from a search index, and an audit event. A checkout request may use the same HTTP endpoint count while creating a database write, an inventory reservation, and a call to a payment provider. If the traffic shifts from search to checkout, the front door can report unchanged requests per second while the database and external provider see different workloads. A useful worksheet therefore names the business operation, the resource it uses, the number of executions at that resource, and which executions happen only when the usual path fails.

Consider an illustrative catalogue with three million lookups per day and a planned peak of 900 lookups per second. Each cache miss makes two backend reads. At an 80% hit rate, the backend reads are 900 × 0.20 × 2, or 360 reads per second. At a 50% hit rate they rise to 900. An empty cache after a rollout would briefly request 1,800 reads per second. Those three rows describe very different operating conditions even though the HTTP forecast is identical. If an isolated backend comfortably sustains 700 reads per second at the latency objective, reducing cache warm-up time or limiting miss throughput deserves attention before raising the public traffic target. Adding more frontends could accelerate the overload.

Estimate reads consumed by probes and background jobs in separate rows. If both the public request and the background warm-up can make cache misses, account for both. Measure hit rates for a fresh deployment, normal operation and an unpopular key. Reporting a single estate-wide hit rate can hide an endpoint whose misses make the most expensive requests. Include a rate where an assumption changes the answer, such as the maximum tolerable number of reads per request, so the next benchmark validates the decision boundary.

## Put time and concurrency on the same boundary

Pick a point where work becomes visible, then define where it leaves. If you count requests in a gateway, use gateway arrival rate and gateway residence time. If you size a connection pool, count connection acquisitions and the time that a connection remains checked out. A request that waits in the gateway before borrowing a connection occupies gateway capacity during that period without occupying a connection. Combining total request latency with connection acquisitions can substantially overstate pool demand.

In an illustrative steady interval, a service completes 600 requests per second. Each request acquires one connection for an average of 25 milliseconds. The average number of connections checked out is 600 × 0.025, or 15. A slow dependency that raises the hold time to 80 milliseconds increases the mean checkout count to 48. This is Little's relationship applied to one boundary. It does not size the pool by itself. An uneven arrival pattern can put many callers in the pool at once while the mean remains low. Record pool wait, hold time, and timeouts together so the next test tells you whether the resource or its queue runs out first. See the connection between work in progress and time through a queue in [MIT's queueing notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/1-203j-logistical-and-transportation-planning-methods-fall-2006/pages/lecture-notes/).

Keeping units explicit also prevents arithmetic drift in bandwidth estimates. At 900 responses per second with 16 KiB of body each, the response payload is 14,745,600 bytes per second, about 118 megabits per second using decimal bits for the line rate. This figure excludes headers, retransmissions and background traffic, and the payload may be measured at a different encoding layer from the network bill. Keep both the conversion and the measurement point in the worksheet.

## Decide what evidence would overturn the model

Calculations become useful reviews when each column has a source and a reason for its uncertainty. A traffic manager may know the expected number of invited recipients while no one knows how many open the link within a minute. Use a range for the conversion, describe its origin, and see whether the minimum and maximum cases point at different designs. If both fit the existing backend, reserve deeper testing for another question. If they straddle a hard limit, rehearse the smallest experiment that resolves it. That may be a cold-cache run against an isolated copy with popular and uncommon keys, rather than a complete production-scale rehearsal.

Keep unobserved work visible. When modeling a dependency, log timeouts, rejections and entry into queues in addition to completed requests. A chart of completed throughput can flatten even as incoming demand and wait grow. A bounded trial that consumes fewer requests than planned needs to distinguish a saturated arrival generator from a service that correctly rejects excess traffic. [Google's SRE guidance on handling overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/) gives examples of choosing a unit of work that reflects actual resource demand and shedding work when necessary.

Save the original prediction and the observed range beside each other. If observed CPU is substantially higher than estimated, inventory extra work per operation before applying a universal safety multiplier. If the forecast misses because cache hit rates decay at peak, carry that dependency into the next design rather than recording only the updated number. The objective is a set of assumptions another reviewer can replace and recompute.

## Price the branch of the decision that changes the bill

One illustrative storage option writes one 4 KiB event per successful update and another writes both the event and a 12 KiB projection. A hundred million successful updates produce roughly 410 GB and 1,638 GB respectively before replication, indexes and overhead. If the projection saves a read only used by one client with limited traffic, that is a narrow benefit buying a recurring storage and write cost. If it replaces an expensive repeat read at high traffic, the direction may reverse. Count rebuilds or replay as their own line because the projection can consume far more provisioned write throughput during a restore than during ordinary operation. Recompute with a shorter retention period if data policy permits it. A reviewer can then see which cost is attached to the architecture and which is attached to a policy the organization could change.
