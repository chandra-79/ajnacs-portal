The promotion to engineering manager usually comes because you were an excellent engineer. The job you're stepping into rewards almost none of those skills directly.

This is not a warning. It's calibration. The transition is disorienting precisely because the feedback loops that told you how you were doing as an engineer — code reviews, shipped features, test results, system metrics — don't exist in the same form. You're doing a different kind of work, and the signals are different.

## What changes

**Your output is no longer code.** Your output is the team — their productivity, their growth, their ability to handle the problems that arise without you in the room. A manager who writes a lot of code is probably doing it at the expense of the team, not in addition to managing it. Code is often the escape hatch from the harder work of developing people and removing organizational obstacles.

**Your time horizon shifts.** As an engineer, you're thinking in sprints and release cycles. As a manager, you're thinking in quarters and half-years. Investments in team processes, relationships, and individual growth take months to show results. The impulse to optimize for visible short-term output (ship more features this sprint) often conflicts with the investments that make the team more capable over time.

**You become a multiplier or a bottleneck.** Every decision you keep to yourself, every task you complete rather than delegate, every process that requires your approval — these are multiplying your leverage in the wrong direction. The goal is to build a team that can operate effectively without needing you for every decision. That requires consciously giving up control of things you could do well yourself.

**Relationships are the work.** One-on-ones, performance conversations, hiring, resolving conflicts, building trust with stakeholders — this is not overhead. This is the job. The manager who views meetings as interruptions to real work has the frame backward.

## What doesn't change

**Technical judgment still matters.** You won't write as much code, but you still need to understand the technical decisions your team makes. Can you evaluate a proposed architecture? Do you understand where the technical risk is? Can you tell when someone is confident versus when they're guessing? These skills atrophy if you don't use them, and they're harder to recover than most people expect.

**Credibility is earned the same way.** As an engineer, you built credibility by delivering. As a manager, you build credibility the same way — by doing what you said you'd do, by following through, by being the person people can rely on. The domain is different (delivering on organizational commitments rather than technical ones) but the mechanism is identical.

**Writing matters more, not less.** Clear written communication — weekly updates, performance feedback, project post-mortems, architecture reviews — becomes more important than it was as an engineer. Your reach expands; your written output is read by more people and carries more weight.

## The first 90 days

The most common mistake in a new management role: trying to make changes before you understand what's actually happening.

The first 30 days should be almost entirely listening. One-on-ones with everyone on the team. Conversations with stakeholders, peers, and your own manager. Sitting in on rituals (standups, planning, retros, incident reviews) without trying to change them. Building a picture of what works, what people are frustrated by, and what nobody is saying aloud.

The questions worth asking in those early conversations:
- What would make your work noticeably easier?
- What decisions take longer than they should?
- What's working well that you're worried might change?
- What would you fix if you could?

By day 30 you'll have a list of improvements. By day 60 you'll have been able to prioritize them with enough context to make good calls. By day 90 you can start making the changes that matter.

Moving before you understand the context is how managers walk into a team that was mostly functional and introduce friction while trying to demonstrate value.

## The performance conversation most managers avoid

Giving clear, specific, timely feedback — especially critical feedback — is the management skill that separates good managers from poor ones more than almost anything else.

Most managers are vague when being specific would feel uncomfortable. "You could improve your communication" instead of "In the last three design reviews, your proposals didn't account for the scaling constraints we've discussed — I need you to include that analysis before bringing proposals to the group."

Vague feedback doesn't change behavior because the recipient doesn't know what to change. It also erodes trust — people can tell when feedback is being softened to the point of uselessness.

The structure that works:
- Specific behavior (what happened, not what the person is like)
- Observed impact (what was the effect of that behavior)
- Expected change (what you need to be different)
- Support offered (what you'll do to help)

This is uncomfortable. Do it anyway. The people who grow fastest are the ones whose managers are honest with them.

## The management skill nobody talks about enough

Protecting your team's time and focus.

An engineering team that is constantly pulled into unplanned work, back-to-back meetings, shifting priorities, and escalated incidents cannot do the deep work that produces good software. Part of your job is to absorb organizational chaos at your layer and shield the team from as much of it as possible.

This means being the person who sits in the unnecessary meetings so your engineers don't have to. It means pushing back on requests that would interrupt the team mid-sprint. It means having the conversation with your stakeholders about what "urgent" actually means and what the cost of interruption is.

Your team's focus is a resource. Manage it accordingly.
