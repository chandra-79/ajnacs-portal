Every data platform has a moment when the investment thesis quietly inverts. The build years assume the constraint is *availability* — get the data in, model it, serve it. Then one morning a VP makes a decision on a dashboard that was silently wrong for three weeks (an upstream schema change, a duplicated load, a timezone shift at the source), the correction is embarrassing, and the organization discovers the real constraint was always *trust* — because a data platform's value isn't the data it holds; it's the decisions people are willing to make on it, and trust, once spent, rebuilds at a fraction of the rate it burns. Data quality engineering is the discipline of spending that budget deliberately: making correctness a *tested, monitored, owned* property rather than an assumption that survives until audited.

## Quality as testable assertions, at the right layers

The vague aspiration ("our data should be accurate") becomes engineering when decomposed into the classic dimensions, each with a concrete test shape: **completeness** (row counts against expected volumes, null-rate ceilings per column, the "did yesterday's partition arrive *at all*" check that catches the silently-dead pipeline), **uniqueness** (grain assertions — the one-row-per-declared-grain tests from dimensional practice, which catch the duplicate-load and fan-out-join bugs that inflate every downstream metric), **validity** (values within domains: enum membership, ranges, format constraints — the schema-shaped checks), **consistency** (cross-dataset reconciliation: does revenue in the warehouse tie to the billing system within tolerance? — the reconciliation-loop pattern from every integration discipline, here as the defense against drift between systems of record and their projections), and **freshness** (below, because it deserves its own economics). The tooling landscape matured into exactly this shape — assertion frameworks embedded in the transformation layer (dbt-style tests running with every build, per the pipeline-gate philosophy: bad data *fails the deploy* rather than reaching the dashboard) plus standalone expectation suites for ingestion edges — and the placement rule mirrors testing practice everywhere: **cheap structural checks at every layer boundary, expensive semantic reconciliations at the critical few** (the revenue pipeline earns cross-system ties; the clickstream sampling checks suffice).

Two disciplines separate mature suites from checkbox suites. **Severity tiers with different behaviors**: blocking failures (grain violations, schema breaks) halt the pipeline; warning-tier anomalies (volume drift, null-rate creep) alert without halting — because a quality system that blocks on everything trains operators to bypass it (the alert-fatigue physics, applied to data), while one that warns on everything is a log nobody reads. **And coverage measured against consumption, not tables**: the question isn't "what fraction of tables have tests" but "do the datasets feeding the top twenty decisions have grain, freshness, and reconciliation coverage" — the criticality-ranked approach every SLO discipline uses.

## Freshness SLOs and the anomaly-detection layer

**Freshness is the quality dimension users feel first** — the dashboard that's *correct but stale* fails its purpose invisibly — and it deserves the full SLO treatment from reliability practice: per-dataset freshness targets ("orders mart current within 2 hours") declared in metadata, measured continuously, alerted by burn-rate rather than single-miss, and *published to consumers* (the staleness-budget honesty from caching and CDC practice — a dashboard footer reading "data as of 09:40" converts silent staleness into informed consumption). Lineage-aware freshness — knowing that the executive mart's lateness *traces to* the upstream extract that stalled — is where the data-observability platforms earn their licenses: the lineage graph from privacy-and-catalog practice, reused as an incident-triage map.

**Anomaly detection** (the ML-flavored layer the vendors lead with) has an honest, bounded role: statistical monitors on volumes, distributions, and null rates catch the *unknown unknowns* your assertions didn't anticipate — the upstream product change that shifted a distribution, the new enum value nobody announced. Treat it as the warning tier's automation, not the foundation: anomaly detection without assertion coverage is a smoke detector in a house with no walls — it fires constantly, attributes nothing, and the false-positive stream burns the exact trust it was bought to protect. Assertions encode what *must* be true; anomalies flag what *changed*; the pairing works, in that order.

## The organizational layer: contracts, ownership, and data incidents

The recurring root cause of data-quality incidents isn't inside the pipeline — it's the *seam*: an upstream team changed a schema, semantics, or cadence without knowing (or owing) anything to downstream consumers. The structural fix is the **data contract** discipline from integration practice, applied at the producer boundary: schemas versioned with compatibility rules, semantic expectations (grain, nullability, enum domains, freshness) declared machine-readably, breaking changes flowing through deprecation windows — with the crucial political corollary that *producers own quality at the source* (the data-mesh ownership argument in its most defensible form: the team that generates the data is the only one who can promise its meaning; downstream cleansing is quarantine, not cure). Where contracts don't yet exist, CDC-fed staging layers with quarantine-and-alert semantics (invalid rows diverted, not dropped — the dead-letter pattern for data) buy the detection while the contract conversation happens.

And when quality fails anyway — it will — **data incidents deserve incident discipline**: severity by decision-impact, a declared owner, consumer notification ("this dashboard is under investigation" banners beat silent wrongness by a wide margin — the status-page instinct, applied inward), root-cause review with the blameless mechanics, and the remediation that data uniquely allows: *backfill and restatement* (the replay-ability superpower from streaming and CDC practice — pipelines designed for reprocessing convert quality incidents from permanent scars into corrected history, with the restatement *communicated*, because a metric that changed retroactively without explanation burns trust twice).

The closing frame for the platform review: measure the trust budget directly — dashboard usage trends (abandonment is distrust's leading indicator), the ratio of decisions citing the platform versus spreadsheet-shadow-IT, incident counts weighted by detection source (found-by-tests versus found-by-VP is the metric that matters most). Data quality engineering, done as above — assertions in the build, SLOs on freshness, contracts at the seams, incidents with owners — is how a platform earns the only compounding asset in analytics: the organizational habit of believing the numbers.
