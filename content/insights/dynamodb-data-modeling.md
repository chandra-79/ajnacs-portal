DynamoDB-style modeling breaks relational engineers in a specific, predictable way: they design entities and relationships first (as trained), then discover the query they need is impossible without a full-table scan, then blame the database. The inversion the Dynamo family demands — and rewards with effectively unlimited, flat-latency scale — is total: **you cannot model the data until you have enumerated the questions.** Relational modeling normalizes entities and lets the optimizer answer arbitrary queries later; Dynamo modeling starts from the access patterns and shapes the table so *each question is a single, indexed, O(1)-ish lookup* — because that's the only kind of question the engine answers well, and that constraint is precisely what makes its scaling promises keepable.

## The physics: partitions, keys, and why scans are a lie

The mental model that explains every rule: a table is a **hash-partitioned key-value store** (the consistent-hashing ring, productized). The **partition key** hashes each item to a partition; the optional **sort key** orders items *within* a partition and enables range queries over them. Everything the engine does cheaply follows: get-by-full-key (one partition, one item), query-within-partition (one partition, a sorted range — `begins_with`, `between`, forward/backward, limited) — and everything it does badly follows too: cross-partition questions (scans touch *every* partition and are priced and paced accordingly — a scan-shaped access pattern in production is a design error wearing an API call), and hot keys (throughput is provisioned *per partition*, so one celebrity tenant/item concentrating traffic hits a per-partition ceiling no table-level capacity fixes — the whale-tenant problem from every multi-tenant discipline, here as a hard physical limit; the mitigations — key salting/sharding hot items, caching in front — are the consistent-hashing skew playbook verbatim).

Item collections (one partition key, many sort-keyed items) are the unit of cheap relational-ish behavior: "give me this customer's recent orders" is a single-partition range query *if* orders live in the customer's partition — which is the design insight the whole discipline builds on.

## Single-table design: pre-joined answers, not entity purity

The famous (and famously confusing) pattern — many entity types in one table with generic `PK`/`SK` attributes — is not aesthetic minimalism; it's **materializing joins at write time**. The relational join ("customer with their orders and addresses") becomes a *layout decision*: store the customer item at `PK=CUST#123, SK=PROFILE`, orders at `PK=CUST#123, SK=ORDER#2026-07-01#...`, addresses at `SK=ADDR#...` — and the "join" is one partition query returning heterogeneous items the application assembles. The sort key becomes a compositional instrument (entity-type prefixes for selective `begins_with`, timestamps for chronology, hierarchical paths for drill-down — the composite-index design rules, played on one string), and item-type discriminator attributes keep the zoo legible.

The honest costs, because single-table design gets sold with too much mystique: the model is **write-time-committed to its questions** (a new access pattern that doesn't fit the key structure is a migration or an index — the schema-on-write of questions, not just fields), the table is **illegible without documentation** (the access-pattern catalog — every question, mapped to its key expression — *is* the schema; maintaining it is non-optional, and its absence is why inherited Dynamo tables feel like archaeology), and **analytics don't belong here at all** (the answer to "sum revenue by region by month" is the CDC-style stream out to the warehouse — the derived-store discipline; Dynamo serves the application's known questions, the lakehouse serves the unknown ones). Modern practice has also relaxed the "single table" absolutism: the real principle is *model item collections around access patterns* — whether that lands in one table or a few is secondary; the anti-pattern was never table count, it was entity-per-table normalization with application-side joins.

## GSIs: every index is a materialized question

Global Secondary Indexes answer the questions your primary key can't — "orders by status," "users by email" — by maintaining a *second copy* of (projected) data under a different key structure. Read them through the systems lens and the operational rules write themselves: a GSI is an **asynchronous, eventually-consistent replica** (read-after-write against a GSI is the replication-lag conversation again — design flows accordingly), it has **its own capacity and its own hot-partition physics** (a GSI keyed on `status` where 90% of items are `ACTIVE` has re-concentrated everything the base table's key spread out — low-cardinality GSI keys are the classic self-inflicted hotspot), and a **throttled GSI back-pressures base-table writes** (the index you added for one dashboard can brown out your write path — capacity-plan indexes like the tables they secretly are). Project only the attributes the question needs (indexes are priced by what they carry), and use the sparse-index trick deliberately: items missing the GSI key simply don't appear — a free filtered view ("only items needing review") that replaces scan-and-filter with a tiny index.

## The judgment layer: when this model earns its constraints

Dynamo-style stores are the right answer when the workload matches the physics: **known, enumerable access patterns** at high scale with flat-latency requirements (the session store, the cart, the profile service, the IoT ingest, the multi-tenant SaaS backbone), operations tolerance for eventual consistency at the edges, and the serverless operational posture as a first-class virtue (no capacity ops, per-request pricing, the DR/replication features as checkboxes). The wrong-fit signals are equally legible: **query flexibility as a requirement** (ad-hoc filters, evolving questions, rich search — that's Postgres with real indexes, or the search-engine tier, or both), **transactional breadth** (Dynamo's transactions exist but are bounded and priced; invariant-heavy domains with cross-entity constraints live more honestly at serializable-capable relational stores — the isolation-levels toolkit needs its tools), and **small scale, frankly** — below the scale where Dynamo's ceilings matter, a boring relational database answers every question you haven't thought of yet, which early products generate weekly (the premature-optimization judgment, applied to storage selection).

The transferable lesson outlasts the product: designing *from access patterns* — enumerating the questions, shaping storage so each is one cheap operation, and paying for flexibility only where you'll use it — is simply what data modeling looks like when the optimizer can't save you. Relational engineers who learn it report the strange side effect: their relational schemas get better too, because "what are the questions?" was always the right first question — SQL just let everyone postpone it.
