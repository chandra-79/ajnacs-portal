The monorepo debate is perennially miscast as a technology argument — Google does it, so it scales; Git chokes on it, so it doesn't — when the technology was never the decision. Repository boundaries are **coordination boundaries**: they determine which changes can be atomic, which dependencies can drift, whose build breaks when you refactor, and what "the latest version" even means. Choose them by how your organization needs changes to flow, and the tooling question answers itself. Choose them by fashion, and no tooling saves you.

## What a monorepo actually buys

One repository holding many projects delivers exactly one superpower, from which everything else derives: **atomic cross-project change.** Rename the shared library's API and update all forty consumers in one commit, reviewed together, tested together, landed together — no version dance, no deprecation window, no consumer left behind on the old signature. From this follow the real benefits: **no internal dependency versioning** (everything builds against head — the diamond-dependency problem and the "which version of utils are we on?" archaeology simply don't exist), **universal visibility and refactorability** (one grep, one IDE index, one place code review sees everything — large-scale refactors and consistent tooling migrations become mechanical rather than multi-quarter diplomatic campaigns), and **one toolchain** (a single CI configuration, lint standard, and dependency policy enforced everywhere by construction).

The costs are the mirror image. **Everything is coupled to head:** your build breaks because a team you've never met landed a change in a library you transitively use — at head, immediately, not at your next version bump. The blast radius of mistakes widens, which is why monorepos *demand* the trunk-based disciplines (fast trustworthy CI, small changes, merge queues) as a survival requirement rather than a virtue. **Tooling stops being free:** past modest scale, naive `git status` and "build everything on every commit" collapse; you need build systems that understand the dependency graph and build/test only what changed (Bazel/Nx/Turborepo-class), CODEOWNERS and path-scoped review to restore ownership boundaries inside the shared space, and eventually virtual-filesystem and sparse-checkout machinery. Google's monorepo works because of a platform-engineering investment most companies cannot and should not replicate — the honest question is never "can a monorepo scale?" but "will *we* fund the tooling ours needs at our size?"

## What polyrepo actually buys

Many repositories deliver the complementary superpower: **independent lifecycles.** Each service/library versions, builds, deploys, and controls access on its own schedule; teams are decoupled by *interface* (published artifacts with versions) rather than coordinated at *source*; blast radii are naturally contained; and the per-repo tooling is the industry default that works out of the box. For organizations whose units genuinely evolve independently — separate products, plugin ecosystems, open-source components, acquisitions, different compliance domains — this is simply the correct model.

Its costs are the monorepo's benefits inverted, and they compound quietly: **cross-cutting change becomes a campaign** (the security fix in the shared library rolls out via forty version-bump PRs across forty repos on forty teams' schedules — with the last five taking a year, which is precisely how known-vulnerable versions live on in production); **version skew is the steady state** (every pair of services meets in production running dependency combinations no CI ever tested together — the integration testing gap that contract testing exists to patch); **discovery and consistency decay** (which repo has the canonical date-handling code? all six of them, differently); and **tooling drift** — each repo's CI, lint, and standards fork gently until "how we build software here" has no single true answer. Polyrepo estates need their own funded machinery to counter this: internal artifact registries with dependency-update automation (Renovate-class bots doing the forty PRs), a platform-owned template/scaffold with drift detection, and org-wide code search — the polyrepo tax is paid in glue, and estates that don't pay it get the decay instead.

## The actual decision framework

Ask where your *coordination pain* lives:

**High internal coupling → monorepo pull.** If your services share many internal libraries, refactors routinely cross project boundaries, and you value fleet-wide consistency (one framework migration, executed once) — the atomic-change superpower is worth real tooling investment. This describes most *product companies with one platform* and most tightly-integrated frontend estates (where the JS ecosystem's workspace tooling has made mid-size monorepos cheap enough to be the default).

**High independence → polyrepo pull.** Genuinely separate products, external/open-source components, strong compliance isolation, teams on different clocks — forcing these into one repo buys coupling nobody wanted.

**Scale changes the math at both ends:** tiny organizations should default to a simple monorepo (one repo, no tooling needed, atomic everything — splitting a five-person startup across twelve repos is process cosplay), and very large organizations converge on *deliberate hybrids* regardless of ideology.

Which is the empirically honest endpoint: most enterprises inhabit the middle — **a few large repos aligned to platform/product boundaries** (the frontend monorepo, the backend services monorepo, the data platform repo) plus satellite repos for the genuinely separate — capturing atomic-change benefits within the boundaries where coupling is real, and lifecycle independence across the boundaries where it isn't. The repo-count religion, in either direction, mostly signals that the coordination analysis was skipped.

Two closing rules give the decision its teeth. First, **repo boundaries should follow ownership-and-coupling boundaries, not team-du-jour org charts** — reorgs are frequent, repo migrations are traumatic; draw the lines on the architecture, which moves slower. Second, **whichever model you choose, fund its tax explicitly**: the monorepo's graph-aware build and merge-queue infrastructure, or the polyrepo's update-automation and consistency machinery. The failure mode common to both isn't the choice — it's choosing the model while skipping its maintenance budget, then blaming the model for the debt. Repository topology is a tool for shaping how change flows through your organization; decide how you want change to flow, and the topology follows.
