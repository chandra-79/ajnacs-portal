"Edge computing" suffers from naming inflation — the term covers everything from a CDN rewriting headers, to serverless functions in 300 cities, to a ruggedized server rack on a factory floor, to inference chips in cameras. The architectures, constraints, and vendors barely overlap; what unites them is one idea: **moving compute toward the data source or the user, because some combination of latency, bandwidth, autonomy, or regulation makes the round trip to a cloud region unacceptable.** The architecture skill is knowing which of those four forces is actually in play — because "edge" adopted for fashion rather than physics is distributed-systems complexity purchased for nothing.

## The tiers, sorted

**The CDN-edge (hundreds of PoPs, milliseconds from users):** edge functions (Cloudflare Workers-class isolates, Lambda@Edge-class functions) executing per-request logic where TLS terminates. Cold starts near zero, execution budgets small, and a constraint that shapes everything: this tier is nearly stateless — its power is *request manipulation*, not application hosting.

**The regional/metro edge (dozens of sites, single-digit-to-low-tens of ms):** carrier and hyperscaler extensions (Local Zones-class, 5G-integrated compute) for workloads needing real servers closer than the region — game servers, real-time media, regulated in-country processing where no full region exists.

**The on-premises/industrial edge (your sites, sub-millisecond, works offline):** gateways and micro-datacenters at factories, hospitals, ships, and stores — where the drivers are less latency than **bandwidth economics** (a vision system generating terabytes daily that the uplink can't carry — filter/infer locally, ship summaries), **autonomy** (the production line must not stop when the WAN does), and **data gravity/regulation** (footage, patient data, sovereign constraints).

**The device edge:** inference on cameras, sensors, vehicles — the extreme of the same logic.

Different tiers, one recurring pattern: **the edge processes, the center consolidates.** Raw signal → local decision + compressed truth upward. Architectures that try to make the edge a *peer* of the center (full application stacks, full data replicas everywhere) inherit the multi-writer distributed-data problem at maximum difficulty; architectures that make the edge a *filter and fast-path* keep the complexity proportional to the benefit.

## What actually belongs at the CDN-edge

The proven residents of the request-path edge, in rough order of value density: **authentication and session validation** (verify the JWT, reject the unauthenticated, route the personalized — before paying the origin round trip); **routing and experimentation** (A/B assignment, canary steering, geo/compliance routing — decisions that are cheap, per-request, and latency-visible); **personalization of cached content** (the cache-plus-edge-assembly pattern: cache the expensive shared page, inject the user-specific fragment at the edge — recovering CDN economics for "personalized" pages); **API shaping** (header hygiene, bot filtering, rate limiting at the front door, response transformation for device classes); and increasingly **rendering** (edge-rendered frameworks executing the view layer close to users, with data fetched from regional backends).

What keeps *not* belonging there, despite annual vendor pushes: heavy business logic that needs the database anyway (moving compute to the edge while the data stays in us-east-1 just relocates your latency from user→compute to compute→data — often a net loss), and anything transactional (below). The test for any edge-function candidate: *does this decision depend only on the request plus small, replicable-to-everywhere state?* If yes, it's an edge natural. If it needs authoritative, mutable, shared state — it's a regional workload wearing an edge costume.

**Edge data** is the frontier where this boundary is being renegotiated: globally-replicated config/flag stores (small, read-heavy, eventually-consistent — ideal), edge caches with regional write-back, and the growing family of edge-replicated databases (read replicas everywhere, writes forwarded to a primary region — good for read-latency, unchanged for writes). The physics, though, doesn't renegotiate: consensus across 300 sites is not a product feature anyone can ship, so **writes at the edge are either forwarded (latency preserved for reads only), partitioned (each datum homed somewhere), or conflict-resolved (CRDT-class semantics for the data shapes that tolerate them)** — the same trilemma as multi-region, multiplied by two orders of magnitude of sites. Design reads-at-edge/writes-at-home first; earn anything fancier.

## The industrial edge: a fleet problem, not a server problem

The on-prem edge's defining challenge isn't the workload — it's **operating thousands of small sites with no on-site engineers**. The disciplines that decide success: **GitOps-style fleet management** (declared desired state per site-class, agents converging, drift visible centrally — the same reconciliation model as Kubernetes estates, and indeed lightweight K8s distributions like k3s-class runtimes have become the standard substrate); **zero-touch provisioning and recovery** (a site must be installable by whoever signs for the box, and recoverable by power-cycling — any procedure requiring a laptop on-site is a design failure at fleet scale); **offline-first semantics** (queue-and-forward for upstream data with local buffering sized for realistic WAN outages, local operation continuing meanwhile — the store must sell things during the ISP's bad day); **physical-world security** (the box can be stolen: disk encryption, hardware-rooted identity, per-site credentials with tight blast radius, and the assumption that any single site may be hostile); and **observability designed for disconnection** (site-local dashboards for the on-site tech, compressed telemetry upward, and central alerting that distinguishes "site offline" from "site unhappy").

## The placement framework

For any workload, walk the four forces honestly: **Latency** — is there a user-experience or control-loop requirement that a regional round trip genuinely violates (measure it; most "we need &lt;10 ms" claims dissolve under measurement)? **Bandwidth** — does moving the computation cost less than moving the data? **Autonomy** — what must keep working during a WAN partition, and what's the honest cost of it not? **Sovereignty** — does law or contract pin the data's location? Workloads scoring zero on all four belong in a region, where everything is easier — and that remains *most* workloads. Workloads scoring on one or more get placed at the *shallowest* tier that satisfies the requirement, with state design (reads distributed, writes homed, truth consolidated centrally) as the first architectural decision, not the last.

The strategic through-line: the edge is not the new cloud — it's the cloud growing a latency-and-locality gradient, and the winning architectures treat it as exactly that: a placement spectrum with physics-driven rules, rather than a destination with a slogan. Put the request-path quick wins at the CDN-edge, the physics-mandated workloads at the industrial edge, keep authoritative state consolidated, operate the fleet declaratively — and let every other workload enjoy the regional cloud's boring, centralized comforts. The edge rewards necessity and punishes fashion, with interest.
