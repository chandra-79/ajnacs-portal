The trap of managing the way you were managed.

Most first-time engineering managers manage the way their best manager managed them. Or worse — the way their most memorable manager managed them, regardless of whether that was actually good.

The habits that made you an effective senior engineer don't automatically translate:
- Solving problems yourself instead of coaching someone through them
- Being the technical authority rather than building the team's technical judgement
- Measuring your own output rather than your team's output

The transition is harder than most people expect, and almost nobody explains it explicitly before you're already in the role.

The clearest signal you've made the transition: you're proud of something your team built that you had nothing to do with.

## Identify the habit before copying it

A practice that helped you as an experienced engineer may not help someone new to the domain. A former manager's light-touch approach may have worked because you already knew the system and its informal escalation paths. Repeating that approach with a new colleague can leave them without the information needed to act. The useful inheritance is a question about what support the person needs, rather than a fixed imitation of the manager's style.

Ask the engineer to describe the next decision, what they know and what remains uncertain. Agree on the boundary within which they can proceed independently. Make review points explicit for irreversible or high-impact actions. Delegation is not the absence of oversight; it is a shared understanding of authority, constraints and escalation.

## A fictional calendar audit

Suppose a new manager spends ten hours each week personally implementing tasks that team members could own with support, and two hours on coaching and planning. In a trial, they reduce that implementation time to four hours and reserve six hours for coaching and planning. Two hours remain available for other responsibilities. The chart compares the ten and four hours of manager-owned implementation; it does not prove the new allocation improves outcomes.

Use the freed time to remove a specific constraint. It might be clarifying acceptance criteria, finding a reviewer or resolving a cross-team priority conflict. If the manager merely attends more meetings while engineers remain blocked, the calendar changed without improving support. Ask for concrete feedback about decisions that became easier and places where the manager is still a bottleneck.

## Practice a coaching conversation

Choose a task that is meaningful but safe to delegate. Ask the engineer to propose an approach and name its risks. Resist supplying the answer immediately. Offer a question or missing piece of context, then ask how it changes the proposal. Agree on an observation that will show whether the approach is working and on a point for checking in.

After the task, discuss the reasoning as well as the result. A sound decision can have an unfavorable outcome under uncertainty, while a risky decision can succeed by luck. Evaluate whether the engineer identified relevant constraints, sought help appropriately and revised the plan when evidence changed. This develops judgment more effectively than rewarding only the final result.

## Adapt your management deliberately

Keep a small list of assumptions about how the team prefers to work. Test them in one-to-one conversations and team discussions. Some colleagues need more context, some need quicker decisions and some need protection from unnecessary intervention. Preferences can change with experience and the situation.

Seek feedback from peers and a manager who can observe your behavior. The transition does not require abandoning technical expertise or refusing to help during an emergency. It requires noticing when personal problem-solving prevents the team from developing capability. A good sign is that decisions remain effective when you are absent, with clear ownership and a reliable route for the exceptions that still need you.
