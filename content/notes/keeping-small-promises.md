“I'll send it later” sounds like a small promise. To the person waiting, it may be the piece that allows tomorrow's work to begin. They might reserve time, delay another decision, or reassure someone further downstream. An informal sentence can become part of several people's plan before its speaker notices the weight it carries.

Keeping small promises is not about being constantly available. It is about making commitments legible enough that other people can use them. A clear refusal can be more dependable than an agreeable answer that never becomes a delivery.

This becomes particularly important in work that is mostly coordination. A review, introduction, approval, or clarification may take little time to perform and still have a large effect on elapsed time. The size of the action does not tell you the size of the dependency.

## Replace atmosphere with a commitment

Friendly language can hide important details. “Soon,” “when I get a chance,” and “I'll look into it” may convey goodwill without creating a shared expectation. Sometimes that is sufficient. If another person must plan around the answer, it is worth being more explicit.

A usable commitment names the deliverable, the time or condition, and the boundary. “I can review the recovery section by Thursday afternoon; I cannot review the full proposal this week” gives the recipient something concrete. It also protects the speaker from accidentally accepting a larger task.

A deadline need not always be a clock time. “After the access request is approved, I will run the check and update the ticket” can be precise if the dependency is observable and someone is watching it. The vague part is not the absence of a date; it is the absence of a shared understanding.

Do not confuse precision with certainty. If the duration is uncertain, say what you can commit to confidently: a first assessment, a progress update, or a decision about whether more work is needed. Promising the next useful piece can be more honest than guessing a completion time.

## An early update is part of delivery

Consider a hypothetical review promised for Friday. On Wednesday, the reviewer discovers an issue that requires specialist input. Waiting until Friday to explain the delay leaves the recipient fewer options. An update on Wednesday allows the plan to change while there is still room to change it.

The update should say what is known, what is blocked, and what happens next. It need not describe every competing demand on the reviewer's calendar. The recipient needs an actionable picture, not a defence brief.

If possible, provide a useful partial result. “The access model is reviewed; the retention section still needs an answer from its owner” can allow other work to continue. Mark the boundary clearly so the partial review is not mistaken for approval of the whole document.

This is also where a promise can be renegotiated. A new date is not established merely because one person announces it. Check whether the revised timing still serves the need. The recipient may prefer a smaller review today or may need to find another reviewer.

## Do not build a reputation on emergency effort

A pattern of accepting too much and rescuing it with late nights can look reliable for a while. The visible outcome is delivery; the invisible cost is the method. That method may be difficult to repeat and impossible for colleagues to plan around.

The alternative is not to expose every private detail of your life. It is to make capacity a legitimate part of the conversation. A commitment should fit alongside existing commitments rather than assume that all of them can expand into the same evening.

The note on [saying no](/notes/on-saying-no/) explores the boundary itself. Here the concern is what happens after saying yes. If every yes depends on unplanned overtime, the commitment process is already borrowing from a resource that nobody has agreed to supply.

This applies to leaders as much as individual contributors. A team should not learn that an estimate is merely the opening position in a negotiation whose final answer is always “faster.” Ask what would need to change to meet the desired date, then decide openly whether that trade is acceptable.

## Make waiting visible without creating surveillance

A simple shared list can prevent promises from disappearing into chat. The list needs an owner, the next action, and a meaningful due condition. It does not need to become a minute-by-minute account of how people spend their day.

The purpose is to expose dependencies and support coordination. If the list becomes a ranking of who replies fastest, people may optimise for visible responsiveness instead of completing difficult work. A quick answer is useful only if it moves the underlying task forward.

Review commitments that repeatedly slip. Perhaps the person is overloaded. Perhaps the deliverable is larger than its label suggests. Perhaps a hidden approval is required. Repeated reminders may keep the symptom moving without revealing why the commitment is unreliable.

Also retire promises that no longer matter. A request can become obsolete while remaining on somebody's private guilt list. Closing it explicitly releases attention and makes the shared record more trustworthy. Completion, cancellation, and transfer are all legitimate outcomes when clearly communicated.

## A weekly check that takes the other person's view

Before the week ends, look through the commitments you made in messages and meetings. For each one, ask who may be waiting and what they believe will happen next. Do not start by asking whether you personally consider the task urgent.

Choose one commitment whose status could surprise its recipient. Send a brief update before the surprise becomes a problem. Clarify the next deliverable and its timing. If you cannot meet the original need, say so while another route is still possible.

Then notice one promise you should have made smaller. Rewrite it as a bounded commitment you could have kept without improvising an evening. This is a planning exercise, not a scorecard for personal worth. The aim is to make tomorrow's promises easier to understand and more likely to hold.

Dependability is built partly through completed work and partly through the quality of the information surrounding unfinished work. People can adapt to uncertainty. They have a harder time adapting to uncertainty presented as a settled promise.
