I have attended a lot of technology conferences. KubeCon, AWS re:Invent, Google Cloud Next, a number of smaller specialist events. The pattern I've noticed is that the value of a conference correlates weakly with its size and strongly with the people you are already going to talk to when you arrive.

For me, most of the value is between sessions.

This is not a criticism of conference talks. Some are excellent, and the best ones — a genuine practitioner sharing hard-won lessons from a production environment, a researcher presenting findings that challenge a common assumption — are worth the time. But the median conference talk is a polished version of information you could have found in a blog post.

The large conference sessions at the major events are often marketing. The vendor-sponsored talks are marketing by definition. Even the community talks are optimized for the format: 40 minutes, a narrative arc, memorable takeaways — which is a format that rewards certain kinds of knowledge (lessons learned, opinions, frameworks) and underserves others (nuance, ambiguity, genuine uncertainty).

What is actually valuable at conferences:

The hallway conversations. The person you end up talking to after a session, or at lunch, who is working on a problem adjacent to yours. These conversations are unstructured, honest, and often produce more insight than the sessions because neither party is presenting — both are thinking aloud.

The people you already know. A conference is a time-boxed excuse to have in-person conversations with people you have been in professional contact with remotely. The investment in those relationships — which compounds over years — is worth the trip cost.

The single workshop or working session where a small group actually works through something together. These are rarer but consistently the highest-value conference format I have encountered. A problem, a room of practitioners, structured discussion. The output is not a slide deck; it is understanding.

What I've stopped doing at conferences: attending sessions because they are on topics I "should" know more about. The sessions that land are the ones where I have genuine context — where I can evaluate the claims, recognize the gaps, and connect what is being said to problems I've actually encountered. Attending sessions to acquire basic literacy on a topic is an inefficient way to learn that topic.

What I've started doing: attending with a shorter list of people I want to talk to, leaving more unscheduled time, and being willing to skip sessions for a good conversation.

The conferences I value most now are smaller, more focused, and less polished. The conversations are more candid when there is not a vendor sponsor to offend. The speakers are more willing to say they are uncertain or that something did not work. The format is less optimized for appearance and more for exchange.

If the choice is between a three-day mega-conference and a two-day focused practitioner gathering, I will take the smaller one most of the time.

## Arrive with one question worth asking

A useful question for a practitioner begins where the talk had to stop. Think about a team that gives a credible account of migrating one service at a time. The published version says the team moved the services without interrupting customer traffic. A question that may produce more understanding asks how the team decided it could switch back, or which two services were harder to separate than the drawing suggested. You are asking about a decision and its constraints. The speaker can answer without giving away customer data or reducing a difficult project to a slogan.

It helps to have enough of your own context to make the conversation two-sided. If you have a working question about a problem, share a short version of what you tried and what remains uncertain. Keep it small enough that the other person can take it in over coffee. Ask for an example, listen to the answer, and resist the impulse to fill the silence with your whole history. There is a difference between finding somebody who can help you think and treating every conversation as a chance to get something from somebody.

Some questions are better answered in private after the event. A practitioner may be comfortable saying a feature was hard to operate and unwilling to describe a specific incident in front of a sponsor, customer, or employer. Give them an easy way to decline the detail. If you intend to turn a conversation into a public note, ask what you can attribute. A technical detail shared informally was shared in that setting. You still have to check whether it belongs in something you publish later.

## Decide what is worth carrying home

A little more time between sessions gives a conversation a better ending. Before going to the next room, write down the question, the idea you heard, and the condition under which it might be useful. If the idea was to run a migration rehearsal a different way, note the assumption that made that approach work. Without that assumption the note can turn into advice you repeat to the wrong team six months later.

Follow-up can be modest. Send an agreed link, share the small example you promised, or pass along a correction if you misremembered a detail in the discussion. The useful follow-up is the one that belongs to the conversation both people actually had. A pile of contacts is easy to collect. A few notes that explain why you would want to continue talking are much easier to use.

Workshops have a useful advantage here. They give participants something shared to remember. In an imagined design exercise, two people can test the same failure case and leave with a specific disagreement about what counts as recovery. That disagreement might be more valuable than a fully agreed presentation, because both know what evidence to look for next. The same standard is useful when deciding whether to attend at all. Ask what the event can give you that would be difficult to get from the recording or a careful reading session. Sometimes the answer is one conversation or one problem that people will work on together. That is enough to put a trip in perspective.
