The kind of work I find most valuable — designing something genuinely difficult, thinking through an architectural problem, writing something that requires sustained attention — does not happen in fragments. It happens in stretches of uninterrupted time where the problem can be held in working memory and turned over until the shape of it becomes clear.

A workday organised around quick replies makes that kind of attention hard to sustain.

The interruptions are not malicious. Slack messages are sent by people who have reasonable questions. Meeting invitations come from people who need to make decisions. The open-plan office was designed by people who genuinely believed that spontaneous collaboration is good (it sometimes is). None of the individual interruptions is unreasonable.

The aggregate is a workday that is mostly fragments.

I've found that the only way to do the work that matters is to be deliberate about protecting the conditions it requires, because those conditions will not exist by default.

What I actually do:

The first 90 minutes of the day are blocked before anything else claims them. Calendar closed. Notifications off. This is not always possible — some days begin with incidents or urgent decisions. But it is the target, and when it happens it is consistently the most productive part of the day.

I work in time blocks rather than task lists for deep work. A task list says "write architecture document." A time block says "9-11am: nothing else exists except this document." The difference in focus is significant.

I am honest with myself about what kind of work requires deep focus and what doesn't. Reviewing a pull request, answering an operational question, providing a quick opinion — these can happen in the fragments. They don't need a protected block. The mistake is treating everything as equally demanding of attention when it isn't.

There is also something about the quality of rest. The ability to sustain deep focus for 2-3 hours depends on actually being rested. Chronic sleep deprivation and overcommitment produce a mental state where sustained focus is not really possible — there is always a background hum of incomplete things demanding attention.

This is partly a time management problem and partly a habit of mind. The background hum is often louder than the actual workload warrants; the anxiety about things undone can be more disruptive than the things themselves. Writing the list, making a decision about what will happen when, and closing those open loops helps more than most productivity tactics.

None of this is original. Cal Newport wrote a whole book about it. What I can add is that the people I know who produce the most interesting work — not the most output, the most interesting work — tend to have worked out some version of this for themselves. They have found the conditions under which they think best and they protect those conditions, even imperfectly.

The protection is imperfect by definition. Work is social; interruptions are sometimes the right answer; not every deep focus session produces something worth the time. But attempting it is still better than not, and the cumulative effect of protecting even 5 hours a week of genuine focus compounds into more than 5 hours of value.

## Give the block a job

Ninety minutes is easier to defend when you can say what the time is for. A calendar entry called focus time tells the team when you are unavailable. A description like testing whether the design survives losing an entire region tells you what to do when you get there. It also gives you a way to tell whether a meeting belongs inside the block. A conversation with the person who understands the recovery process might be exactly what the work needs. Another status call can wait.

The beginning can be unremarkable. Re-read the last assumption you wrote down. Draw the part of the system you still cannot explain. Work through one failure and see where the diagram stops answering the question. A useful block might end with a failed idea and a reason to put it aside. That outcome is less visible than a finished document and can save a great deal of polishing on the wrong design. It helps to decide in advance what evidence would count as progress.

The end deserves a small deliberate act too. Leave a note with the question you have open, the last thing you tried, and the first thing to do when you return. If you are working through a set of logs, capture the exact interval and the observation that made it interesting. Someone who has to switch to an incident can do the same thing in two lines. The next block then starts inside the problem rather than at the beginning of the search for it.

## Make it possible for people to find you

Protected time has to fit around obligations other people can reasonably expect you to honour. A person on call needs a clear path for urgent issues. A team that shares support work can move questions through an agreed channel and nominate someone to look at it. A colleague who needs a decision before they can continue should know when you will reply. The arrangement works best when the people carrying the interruptions helped decide how to handle them. Otherwise your quiet morning can mean somebody else's busier one.

Consider an ordinary example. A team has a 90-minute design block and an afternoon overlap with a partner group. The team could arrange a small decision window after the block, leave a written question before closing chat, and make an agreed exception for live service trouble. If the partner team has a decision deadline halfway through the block, move the block or agree on a delegate. The work benefits from certainty about when the decision happens whichever arrangement the teams choose.

An experiment here can be brief. Pick two comparable days and protect a block on one of them. Write down what you were able to finish or understand and who had to wait for you. Look for evidence in the work itself. A cleaner design explanation is more informative than a number of undisturbed minutes. If somebody was needlessly blocked, fix the coverage arrangement. If the block was long and the work stayed shapeless, the question may have been too broad. Change one part and try again.

The arrangement should be easy to revise. Some weeks call for people to be available to each other almost continuously. During those weeks a short, shared block may be enough to make one hard decision. On a quieter week there may be room to work on two. The point of making the time visible is to decide which sort of week you are having before it has happened to you.
