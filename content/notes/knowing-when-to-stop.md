There is always another improvement available. A paragraph can be clearer, a diagram more elegant, a test more comprehensive, a presentation more polished. The existence of an improvement does not tell you whether making it is the best use of the next hour.

Stopping can feel uncomfortable because it makes the boundary visible. The work is released with known limits. Someone else may see an awkward detail. A future requirement may reveal that a choice could have been different. Endless refinement postpones that encounter, but it also postpones the value the work was meant to provide.

Finishing requires a judgment about sufficiency. That judgment should come from purpose, consequences, and evidence rather than exhaustion alone. “Enough” can be a disciplined conclusion when the conditions for enough are clear.

## Decide what the work must enable

A task becomes difficult to finish when its purpose keeps expanding. A short guide becomes a complete reference. A prototype becomes a product. A review becomes a redesign. Each expansion may sound useful, but together they can remove the original stopping point.

Write what the intended reader or user should be able to do after receiving the work. Then identify the conditions that make that action safe and meaningful. A recovery guide should let an authorised operator recognise the relevant failure and follow a tested path. It does not need to teach the entire history of the platform.

A fictional training handout might need a working setup, one complete exercise, a troubleshooting section for expected problems, and a clear route for further study. Adding every advanced topic could make it less usable for the first session. More content is not automatically more help.

Scope is not a defence against necessary work. If testing reveals that the guide omits a prerequisite, that gap belongs inside the task because the intended action depends on it. The useful distinction is between what enables the promised outcome and what extends the ambition beyond it.

## Separate a defect from an enhancement

A defect prevents the work from meeting its stated purpose or introduces an unacceptable consequence. An enhancement adds value beyond that boundary. The distinction can be debated, but making it explicit is better than letting every suggestion enter the same endless queue.

For a public article, a materially incorrect claim needs correction before publication. A slightly different illustration may be an enhancement. For a form, losing the user's text after a failed submission may be a defect; adding another animation is likely something else.

Some issues sit between these categories. An explanation may technically be correct but so confusing that readers cannot use it. A design may function but exclude people who need a different interaction. Assess the actual consequence rather than classifying by whether the change appears cosmetic.

Keep a short list of deliberate limitations. State what the work covers, what it does not, and which condition would justify returning. This makes stopping reviewable. It also prevents a known limitation from being rediscovered later as if nobody had considered it.

## Use a final pass with a different question

During creation, the question is often “How can this be better?” A final pass needs a more bounded question: “Does this now do what it promises, and is there a consequential reason not to release it?” The second question can still reveal serious work, but it does not invite unlimited expansion.

Check the important path from beginning to end. Follow the instructions, open the links, run the example, or ask a representative reader to explain the conclusion. A final pass should test use, not only appearance.

Then inspect the most likely failure path. What happens when a prerequisite is missing, an input is invalid, or a reader needs clarification? The appropriate depth depends on the artifact. A note and a production change do not have the same verification burden.

Do not confuse repeated checking with increasing evidence. Running the same check again without a change or unresolved concern may provide reassurance but little additional information. Once the necessary checks pass, the next responsible action may be delivery.

## Leave a route back

Stopping is easier when revision is possible and ownership is clear. A document can identify its current version and correction route. A release can have a rollback plan. A small experiment can record the condition that would justify a larger one.

This does not eliminate the need for care before delivery. Some actions are difficult to reverse, and their review should reflect that. But treating every artifact as irreversible can make ordinary work unnecessarily heavy.

A route back should not be an excuse to ship a known harmful defect. It is a way to manage the uncertainty that remains after proportionate checking. The distinction matters: “we can fix it later” is not a complete argument when someone else bears the avoidable cost now.

When a later improvement is genuinely useful, give it a place and a reason. A backlog item should identify the value it would add or the condition that would make it necessary. Otherwise the backlog becomes a museum of possibilities that nobody has chosen.

## Write a stopping note

For one task that keeps expanding, write three sentences. Name the outcome it now enables. State the evidence that the important path works. Describe the most significant remaining limitation and the condition for revisiting it.

Ask whether another hour would remove a consequential problem or merely make the work feel more comfortable to release. If it would remove a problem, do that work. If not, deliver the artifact with its boundary visible and move to the next responsibility.
