I read a lot — always have. But for most of my career, I read in the same way I approached most things: with efficiency as the primary goal. Get through the book, extract the relevant ideas, move on.

I got through more books that way and remembered less of what I read. You can have read fifty books in a year and have fewer actual ideas from them than someone who read ten slowly and sat with what they found.

The change started when I noticed I couldn't reliably articulate the ideas from books I'd read eighteen months ago. Not the titles, not the authors — those I remembered. The actual content had passed through and left no lasting impression. That seemed like a problem with the practice rather than with my memory.

The things I changed:

**I stopped keeping up with the pace of technical publication.** There is an infinite amount of technical content to read. The value of staying current diminishes quickly — most newly published material is incremental. I read fewer technical books now and read them more carefully. Selectivity about what counts as worth the attention is the higher-order skill.

**I started taking notes with actual synthesis in them, not just highlights.** Highlights capture what the author said. Synthesis notes force you to say what the idea means in your own words, which is when you find out whether you understood it or just recognised it. That distinction matters. Recognition without understanding is common and easy to mistake for learning.

**I started reading things I disagree with.** The books that have changed my thinking the most haven't been the ones that confirmed what I already thought. They've been the well-argued cases for positions I initially resisted. The discipline of engaging seriously with those — rather than reading for ammunition against the argument — produces something useful. I don't always change my mind. But I understand the territory better.

**I started reading more slowly in the evenings.** Not as a time-management strategy — as a deliberate change of register. Evenings spent reading fiction or history rather than technical material are not time away from learning. They're a different kind of input: narrative, slower, less instrumentally structured. The brain needs that.

The thing I still struggle with: reading without the phone nearby. The gravitational pull of the notification is irrational relative to the value of what it delivers, and I know this, and I still get pulled in. That's an ongoing practice rather than a solved problem.

What I'd say to past me: read less but better. The idea that you need to have read everything is a trap. The relevant ideas in any field can be absorbed from a fraction of the total publication output. Be more selective, more deliberate, and more willing to stay with one thing until it changes how you think.

## Stay with one paragraph long enough to explain it

It helps to give the reading a small practical test. Imagine a book argues that the way to manage a delay is to start fewer things at once. The claim can sound reasonable enough to highlight without thought. Close the book and try to explain which delay it is talking about. Is a person waiting for work to start, waiting for somebody else's decision, or watching a task proceed slowly once begun? Think of a situation where a different intervention would make more sense. If you cannot draw the distinction, leave the note as a question and return to the page.

A synthesis note can name the claim in one sentence and give it a specific example in the next. Add the page or passage so you can find the argument again. Then say what has to be true for the example to count. This is a smaller thing than a summary of a book. It may be the only part of the book worth returning to for a particular problem. The unfinished version is useful too. A line about the two interpretations you are still choosing between gives you something to read for next time.

One reason to include an example from outside the book is that it makes agreement less automatic. The examples the author selected were there for a reason. Try the idea against a situation that the author did not choose. A case where it fails can make the idea clearer without settling whether the book is right overall. You come away with a rule that has a visible limit rather than a sentence you are likely to repeat in every circumstance.

## Leave an easy way back in

Suppose the phone keeps interrupting a technical chapter. An attainable experiment is to put it in another room until you reach the end of one section. Start with the question you want the section to answer. At the end, write what changed about the question. Then decide whether to carry on, go back, or leave the book. The length of the session matters less than whether it produced an idea you can recover tomorrow.

The next day's check can be brief. Write down the idea before opening the note. If the wording has faded but you can still explain the example and what it demonstrates, the thought has stayed with you. If you can remember only which sentence you liked, look back and decide whether understanding it is worth another attempt. Some passages can remain enjoyable without becoming part of your working knowledge. There is no obligation to extract a use from everything you read.

The same goes for disagreement. Write the strongest account of the author's position before setting down your objection. A reader who can make both views intelligible has done more useful work than one who has collected a dozen convenient quotations. There is also a way to read fiction or history that allows a passage to be strange without immediately asking what it teaches. Let it have that time. Some things deserve to stay in mind for a while before you decide what to make of them.
