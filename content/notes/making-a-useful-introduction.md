An introduction can look like a very small act: two names, a few lines, and a message sent. Yet it creates work for both people. They must understand why they have been connected, decide whether to respond, and determine what the next conversation is supposed to accomplish. A generous intention does not automatically make that work easy.

A useful introduction provides context without attempting to script the relationship. It explains the possible connection, gives each person room to choose, and avoids promising more than either has agreed to offer. It treats access to someone's attention as something to handle carefully rather than a resource the introducer owns.

This matters in professional communities, where being helpful can become entangled with being well connected. The impressive part of an introduction is not the number of recognizable names copied into a message. It is whether the people involved can quickly see a worthwhile next step and feel free to decline it when the timing or fit is wrong.

## Begin with a reason small enough to explain

Consider a hypothetical situation in which one person is exploring how to document a complex system and another has recently worked through a similar documentation problem. The connection may be useful. But saying that they should meet because they are both interesting people places the burden of finding that usefulness on them. A clearer reason names the shared question and the kind of experience that might help.

The reason should also be honest about the limits. Similar topics do not mean identical circumstances. A person who has worked on one system is not automatically an adviser on every related problem. Explain the overlap without turning it into an endorsement of expertise you have not assessed. The introduction can open a conversation without guaranteeing its answer.

Ask whether an introduction is welcome before creating a new shared thread, especially when it involves a request for time, a job opportunity, or sensitive context. Consent is useful for practical reasons as well as courtesy. Someone may be unavailable, may have a conflict, or may prefer a different contact route. A brief check can prevent an awkward refusal in front of a third person.

Do not attach private information merely because it makes the introduction more persuasive. A person's job search, financial situation, health, or frustration with their current work may not be yours to disclose. Share only the context they have agreed can be shared. An introduction should not make someone spend its first minutes correcting an unwanted story about themselves.

## Keep the request proportionate

A request to answer one question differs from a request to mentor someone indefinitely. A short exchange about a resource differs from a review of an entire business plan. Be explicit about the initial ask. The relationship may grow later, but it should not begin with a hidden commitment disguised as a friendly connection.

It is often useful to provide a concrete artifact or question. A short description of the problem, a public article, or a clearly scoped draft gives the recipient something to evaluate. Avoid forwarding a large collection of material without explaining what matters. More context is not always better context; the relevant details should make the decision easier rather than create a reading assignment.

The introducer should avoid deciding the next meeting on behalf of both people. Suggest a possible route, then let them choose. One may prefer an email response, another a brief call, and another a referral to a published resource. A useful response does not have to resemble the interaction the introducer imagined.

There is also no need to exaggerate either person's achievements. Inflated praise can make the exchange uncomfortable and create expectations that are hard to meet. A concrete description of relevant work is usually stronger. It explains the basis of the connection and gives the people involved room to speak for themselves.

## Leave without disappearing from responsibility

After making the connection, clarify whether you are stepping out of the thread or remaining available for context. This small detail prevents uncertainty about who is expected to reply. The introduction should not require both people to maintain a performance for the person who introduced them. Once its purpose is clear, their conversation belongs to them.

At the same time, remain responsible for claims you made. If you described the request incorrectly or omitted an important constraint, help correct it. Stepping out should not mean leaving others to resolve a misunderstanding you created. A good introduction is light in its ongoing demands, not careless about its initial framing.

For a reflection exercise, draft an introduction you might reasonably make without sending it. Write the purpose in one sentence, the relevant context in another, and the initial ask in a third. Then mark every claim that requires permission or verification. If the draft depends on private information or an assumed commitment, revise it before involving anyone.

Now imagine receiving the message during a busy week. Can you tell why you are included? Can you understand the scale of the request? Is there a graceful way to decline? If those answers are unclear, the introduction may be convenient for the sender while inconvenient for the recipients. The revision should reduce that imbalance.

Finally, ask whether an introduction is needed at all. Sometimes a public resource, an existing community channel, or a concise answer is enough. Connecting people can be valuable, but it should not be the automatic response to every question. The best form of help may be one that uses less of everyone's time while still meeting the need.

The note on [professional conferences](/notes/on-conferences/) considers what makes participation in a community worthwhile. Thoughtful introductions are one small part of that participation. They turn familiarity into a useful connection while respecting the people whose attention makes the connection possible. That is a quieter skill than collecting contacts, and a more durable one.
