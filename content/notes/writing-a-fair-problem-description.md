The way a problem is described shapes who can help with it. A statement such as people do not care about documentation points toward motivation and character. A statement that new users cannot find the prerequisite before reaching a failing step points toward a particular experience. The second description may still lead to difficult questions about ownership, but it gives the conversation somewhere concrete to begin.

Fairness does not require softening a serious problem. It requires describing the problem accurately enough that the people involved can examine it. A vague accusation can be both harsh and technically unhelpful. A precise account can be direct about consequences without pretending to know more about motives than the evidence supports.

A useful problem statement also leaves room for more than one solution. If the problem is written as we need a new portal, the proposed remedy has already replaced the difficulty it is meant to address. The team may spend its time evaluating portals without asking whether the underlying issue is missing ownership, poor navigation, or conflicting information. The framing has quietly selected the project.

## Begin with an observable gap

Imagine a hypothetical support process in which requests are sometimes left without a named owner. A fair description would identify where ownership becomes unclear, how the gap was observed, and what consequence follows. It might say that a sample of recent requests required additional messages to determine who would respond. It should not claim that every request is mishandled unless the evidence supports that scope.

The desired state belongs beside the observation. What should happen instead, and why? Every request may need a visible owner within an agreed interval. The requirement should be clear enough to test, while remaining open about how to meet it. A shared queue, a routing rule, or a smaller responsibility change might each address the gap.

Include the people affected without speaking for all of them. A problem may look different to the requester, the responder, and the person maintaining the process. Gather enough of those perspectives to avoid making one view stand for the entire system. When a perspective is missing, say so. An honest gap in context is more useful than an invented consensus.

Describe the consequence at the level you can support. Extra coordination, delayed completion, repeated clarification, or a specific failure are concrete. A sweeping claim about lost trust may require more evidence. The seriousness of a problem does not increase merely because its description uses larger words. Precision helps people decide what response is proportionate.

## Remove the solution hidden inside the complaint

Read the statement for phrases that already prescribe an answer. We need more automation may conceal a recurring manual task. We need better communication may conceal an unclear handoff. We need a single source of truth may conceal conflicting records with different owners. Translate the phrase back into the observed difficulty before deciding whether the proposed remedy fits.

This does not mean solutions must be excluded from the discussion. A person may have a well-supported recommendation. Present it as a recommendation after the problem, with its assumptions and tradeoffs visible. The separation allows others to agree that the problem matters while questioning whether the first proposed answer is the right one.

Look for absolute language too. Always, never, nobody, and everyone can make a statement easy to challenge even when its central concern is valid. Replace them with the actual scope: the observed cases, a defined workflow, or a particular period. This keeps the discussion from becoming an argument over one counterexample while the recurring difficulty remains untouched.

Be careful with labels such as careless, resistant, or unprofessional. They may express frustration, but they do not explain the mechanism. What action was missing? What expectation was unclear? What constraint prevented the desired behavior? If a conduct concern genuinely needs to be addressed, it can be addressed directly with appropriate evidence. It should not be smuggled into a technical problem statement as an assumed explanation.

## Give the statement a boundary

A problem becomes easier to investigate when its scope is visible. Name the process, users, environment, and period under discussion as far as they matter. Also name what is outside the current question. Without a boundary, a small issue can expand into a general diagnosis of the organization, and every proposed improvement can appear inadequate because it does not fix everything.

A boundary is not a promise to ignore related problems. It is a way to begin somewhere testable. If the investigation reveals a broader cause, update the scope and explain why. The original statement should be a working tool that can change with evidence, not a document whose authority depends on remaining unchanged.

For a practical exercise, take a complaint you have written or heard recently. Rewrite it in four sentences: what was observed, what should have happened, why the difference matters, and what remains unknown. Do not name a solution in those sentences. Then list one possible explanation and one alternative explanation. Notice whether the problem becomes easier to discuss when the cause is no longer assumed.

Ask someone close to the work whether the description is recognizable and fair. Invite corrections to the facts and scope before discussing remedies. This does not give every participant a veto over uncomfortable evidence. It gives the statement a chance to become accurate enough that disagreement can focus on interpretation and action rather than a distorted account of what happened.

The note on [feedback](/notes/on-feedback/) considers how an observation becomes useful to another person. A fair problem description applies that care to shared work. It makes the difficulty specific, keeps motives separate from evidence, and offers a question that several people can help answer.

A well-written problem statement does not solve the problem. It makes solving possible without first requiring everyone to defend themselves against an imprecise story. That is meaningful work. It replaces a complaint that circulates with a question that can be investigated, revised, and eventually answered through a concrete change.
