Feedback goes wrong in both directions. We soften it until it is hard to act on, or we hear it as an attack and miss the information in it.

I've been on both sides of this enough times to have some views.

The problem with soft feedback is not that it is kind. Kindness in difficult conversations is good. The problem is when kindness is used to avoid specificity, and the result is feedback that the recipient cannot act on.

"Your communication could be stronger" is not feedback. It is a category. Feedback is: "In the last two architecture reviews, you presented your conclusions without the supporting analysis. The stakeholders in the room couldn't evaluate whether your recommendation was sound. I need you to include the trade-off analysis in the presentation itself, not just in your head."

The second version is uncomfortable to give. It is also the version that produces change.

On receiving feedback: the instinctive response to criticism is to defend. To explain why the situation was more complicated, why the other party misunderstood something, why the same choice would be made again. This is almost always the wrong first move.

The more productive first move is curiosity. What is the experience the other person had that prompted this feedback? What were they hoping for that didn't happen? Is this a pattern they've observed or a single instance?

None of this requires agreeing with the feedback. You can be curious about the experience someone had and conclude, after considering it carefully, that the feedback reflects a misunderstanding or a different set of priorities. But the curious response produces better information than the defensive one.

The feedback I've found most useful over the years has often been the most uncomfortable to receive. Not because criticism is inherently valuable, but because the feedback that is comfortable to give — generic, positive, easy — tends to be low-information. The feedback that requires courage from the giver tends to be specific, accurate, and about something that matters.

There is a version of professional life where you surround yourself with people who only tell you what you are doing well. It feels comfortable. It is not how you get better.

One thing I try to do that I found in an article years ago and haven't let go of: when I receive feedback that stings, I give myself 24 hours before responding substantively. The initial sting is often emotional. The 24-hour version of the same feedback is usually more informative and easier to evaluate on its merits.

This is harder to do in live conversation, but even then — acknowledging the feedback, saying you'll think about it, and following up — is better than the defensive response generated in the moment.

## Establish what happened while the details are fresh

Before a difficult conversation, try writing the example in a form a person who was there could confirm. An architecture review that ended without a decision is observable. Calling its presenter unprepared is an interpretation. The interpretation may eventually be warranted, but it can wait. Start with what the people in the room needed to do and where they ran out of help. Perhaps they had a recommendation but nobody knew the cost of the alternative. Perhaps the actual decision maker had expected a short check-in and was not prepared to decide. Those are different problems. Either might explain why the meeting ended the same way.

Receiving that account well can mean finding an overlooked fact. Suppose the presenter shared the comparison two days earlier, and two of the people doing the evaluation never saw it. That matters to the next review. The presenter can still make the alternatives visible in the room. The team can also make sure the right people receive them before the discussion. A useful feedback conversation leaves space for both changes. Turning it into a case for who was right makes that harder.

## Give the next attempt a clear test

The next action should be something the recipient can recognise when it is done. Asking for a stronger executive presence gives them an impression to manage. Asking for the decision, the main trade-off, and the person who owns the next step in the first five minutes gives them something to try. The review can then look at whether people could make the decision earlier. If they still could not, the team has found the next question.

There is a corresponding obligation for the giver. If you agreed to look at the next draft before the meeting, look at it. If the work has to be done differently, say what changed. Specific feedback earns trust when the giver is willing to help test the result. Otherwise the recipient can spend a great deal of effort guessing whether the works-in-this-room version of the advice still applies at all.

The pause before responding is useful when it gives you time to separate the sting from the information. A pause works better with a date on it. Tell the other person when you can return to the conversation, then show up with the example as you understand it, a question about what you are missing, and one thing you can try. If the feedback touches on repeated harm or a safety concern, waiting to address the underlying problem may have a different cost. First make sure the problem is acknowledged and someone is dealing with it. Ordinary disagreement about an architecture review rarely needs the same speed.

There will still be times when you disagree. It helps to be able to say where the disagreement lives. You might share the other person's account of what happened and differ about the right response. The conversation has already made something clearer if you can say that without guessing at motives.
