You spend weeks deciding what furniture to sell and what to ship. You agonise over boxes, cubic feet, insurance riders. And then you arrive and realise the things that cost you nothing to carry — habits, assumptions, the particular way you read a room — are the ones that need the most unpacking.

Moving countries is less like relocating and more like doing a hard restart on a lot of invisible settings.

The obvious stuff adjusts fast. New SIM, new bank account, new commute. Within a month you know which supermarket to trust and which coffee shop has the right ratio of quiet to ambient noise for getting work done.

The slow stuff is subtler. How direct to be in a meeting. When a "yes" means yes versus when it's a polite holding pattern. How much silence is comfortable in a conversation before someone feels obligated to fill it. These things you learn by getting them slightly wrong, repeatedly, until one day you notice you stopped getting them wrong.

What I didn't expect was how much I'd miss things I never consciously appreciated. Not obvious things — not family or food, those are easy to name. Specific things. The particular quality of evening light at a certain time of year. The sound of a specific language being spoken loudly and argumentatively in a market and somehow feeling like warmth rather than noise.

The strange gift of it is that you start to see your original home more clearly from far away. Things you assumed were universal turn out to be local. Things you thought were personal quirks turn out to be cultural. It's disorienting and clarifying at the same time.

I've moved enough times now that I've stopped expecting the new place to eventually feel like home in the way the old place did. It won't. But it becomes its own kind of home — different geography, different light, different silences. You just accumulate more of them.

What you actually pack, it turns out, is yourself. Everything else is logistics.
