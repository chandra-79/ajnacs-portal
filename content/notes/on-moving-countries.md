You spend weeks deciding what furniture to sell and what to ship. You agonise over boxes, cubic feet, insurance riders. And then you arrive and realise the things that cost you nothing to carry — habits, assumptions, the particular way you read a room — are the ones that need the most unpacking.

Moving countries is less like relocating and more like doing a hard restart on a lot of invisible settings.

The obvious stuff adjusts fast. A new SIM, a new bank account, a new commute. Within a month you know which supermarket to trust and which coffee shop has the right ratio of quiet to ambient noise for getting work done.

The slow stuff is subtler. How direct to be in a meeting. When a "yes" means yes versus when it's a polite holding pattern. How much silence is comfortable in a conversation before someone feels obligated to fill it. These things you learn by getting them slightly wrong, repeatedly, until one day you notice you stopped getting them wrong.

What I didn't expect was how much I'd miss things I never consciously appreciated. Not obvious things — not family or food, those are easy to name. Specific things. The particular quality of evening light at a certain time of year. The sound of a specific language being spoken loudly and argumentatively in a market and somehow feeling like warmth rather than noise.

The strange gift of it is that you start to see your original home more clearly from far away. Things you assumed were universal turn out to be local. Things you thought were personal quirks turn out to be cultural. It's disorienting and clarifying at the same time.

I've moved enough times now that I've stopped expecting the new place to eventually feel like home in the way the old place did. It won't. But it becomes its own kind of home — different geography, different light, different silences. You just accumulate more of them.

What you actually pack, it turns out, is yourself. Everything else is logistics.

## Notice who can explain the unfamiliar parts

One useful person after a move is somebody willing to explain the small things that everybody else assumes you already know. Think of a new colleague watching a discussion end without a clear decision. In their previous workplace that might have meant people wanted more evidence. Here it might mean the decision belongs to someone who was absent. The two readings lead to different next moves. A quiet question about who normally decides can clear up something no amount of replaying the meeting in your head will settle.

You can also ask a narrower question than what a whole country is like. How does this office show disagreement? When this person asks you to follow up, do they want a decision or more information? The answers may vary from one team to another in the same city. Staying with a particular moment gives you something you can test at the next one. It also keeps you from explaining a stranger's ordinary bad day as a lesson about the place.

## Allow for things worth keeping

Adaptation raises a smaller and more interesting question than how to fit in. It asks which habits are still useful where you are. A familiar way of welcoming people may work quite well after a little translation. A habit of waiting to be invited into a conversation might be worth changing if nobody knows you are waiting. Either decision makes more sense when you can see the effect it has on the people around you. You can revise a habit without treating the place you learned it as a mistake.

Consider a first week when a person feels that nothing is settled. Choosing one place to return to or making one ordinary plan with somebody nearby does not tell them what kind of home this will become. It gives them a second occasion to see the same faces under different conditions. Over time, there may be places they know well enough to stop comparing them with what they used to have. The old comparison can still be there when it matters. The new place has started giving them things worth noticing on its own terms.
