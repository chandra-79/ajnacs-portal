An extraordinary effort can deserve gratitude. Someone stays to resolve a difficult incident, helps a colleague through an unexpected problem, or protects a delivery when a real emergency arrives. Recognising that contribution is reasonable.

The difficulty begins when the organisation plans as if extraordinary effort were an ordinary input. The same person repeatedly rescues the release. The same team quietly absorbs the missing capacity. What looks like commitment from a distance may be a process depending on a fragile and unacknowledged condition.

Good work should be able to survive a normal week. That does not mean every week will be calm. It means the plan should not require someone to become exceptional on demand in order for routine obligations to be met.

## Ask what the rescue is compensating for

A recurring rescue deserves investigation. Is the scope routinely larger than the available capacity? Does a critical decision arrive late? Is one person the only holder of a permission or piece of knowledge? Does a brittle procedure fail in a predictable way?

Consider a fictional release that depends on a senior engineer manually reconciling configuration differences every Friday. The engineer's care may be preventing failures. Praising that care without examining the repeated mismatch leaves the underlying dependency intact.

The response should not be to withdraw help abruptly or blame the person for being indispensable. Make the work visible, learn how the reconciliation is performed, and identify which part can be removed, documented, or checked earlier. Protect the current service while reducing the need for future rescue.

The [SRE toil guide](/insights/sre-toil-reduction/) gives a practical route for recurring operational effort. The relevant question is whether the task keeps returning without producing an enduring improvement, and what intervention could change that pattern safely.

## Preserve gratitude without rewarding the wrong system

A team can thank the person who helped and still say that the circumstances should not repeat. These are compatible messages. The contribution was valuable; the dependence on that contribution may be unhealthy for the work.

Recognition should include the people who make rescues less necessary. The colleague who tests a recovery procedure, clarifies an ownership boundary, or removes a fragile manual step may prevent a future crisis without creating a memorable emergency story.

If recognition follows only visible emergencies, ordinary preventive work can become difficult to justify. People learn what receives attention. Leaders should explain why an uneventful release or a clean handover reflects useful engineering and coordination rather than an absence of effort.

Avoid assigning moral labels to working patterns. A person who leaves at an agreed time is not necessarily less committed than someone who stays. The important question is whether responsibilities are met within a sustainable and explicit arrangement, not whether devotion is publicly displayed through exhaustion.

## Make recovery a team capability

When one person knows the recovery path, the system's resilience includes that person's availability. Documentation can help, but a document nobody has followed is weak evidence that the capability has been shared.

Use a safe rehearsal in which another authorised person follows the procedure. Observe where they need private knowledge, an undocumented permission, or an interpretation that only the original expert can provide. Improve those points and repeat the exercise at a proportionate interval.

Do not expect a document to replace every form of expertise. Some situations require judgment and escalation. The procedure should identify that boundary and provide a reliable route to help. Shared capability includes knowing when to stop and who can take the next decision.

[Google's postmortem guidance](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/) offers an approach to examining incidents through system conditions and follow-up work. A useful review asks what made the rescue necessary and what would make a similar event easier to handle next time.

## Give the plan an honest capacity

A plan can contain more work than the team can perform without ever stating that fact. Estimates are compressed, dependencies are ignored, and every task is described as small. The deficit appears later as a series of urgent personal favours.

Make the trade visible earlier. If the desired date and full scope cannot both fit, decide whether to reduce scope, move the date, add appropriate capacity, or change the approach. Adding people is not an instant solution when the work requires scarce context or coordination, so examine the actual constraint.

Leave room for the responsibilities that already exist. Support, maintenance, teaching, and review do not vanish because a project plan omits them. A schedule built from only the most visible development tasks can be unrealistic while appearing carefully detailed.

The people doing the work should be able to challenge the assumptions without being asked to prove their commitment. An estimate is information about the plan, not a test of loyalty. The earlier that information can be used, the less often the team needs a rescue to hide the mismatch.

## Review the last repeated emergency

Choose a problem that has required extraordinary effort more than once. Write down the trigger, the people involved, the missing capability, and the condition that made the response difficult. Use the actual records rather than reconstructing the event only from its most dramatic moment.

Identify one change that would reduce the need for the same intervention. Assign an owner and a way to check whether the change worked. If the change requires capacity, put that capacity into the plan instead of assuming the rescuers will find another evening for it.

Then recognise both the response and the repair. The accompanying diagram connects the emergency to the system condition and the next rehearsal. Its purpose is to move appreciation beyond the moment of rescue.

There will still be difficult days. A dependable team does not eliminate every surprise. It makes fewer ordinary commitments depend on someone repeatedly exceeding ordinary limits, and it treats the ability to recover as shared work rather than a personal legend.
