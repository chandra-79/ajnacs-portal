A standard can be expressed as a wall or as a description of what the work needs. The wall tells someone that their attempt is not good enough. The description explains which requirement is unmet, why it matters, and what would count as an adequate revision. Both can preserve a boundary. Only one reliably gives the person something useful to do next.

Kindness is sometimes treated as a softening of standards, as though a reviewer must choose between accuracy and consideration. That choice is too narrow. It is possible to reject unsafe code, an unsupported claim, or an incomplete analysis while treating the author with respect. The difficulty is doing the intellectual work required to make the rejection precise.

A vague dismissal transfers that work to the person receiving it. They must infer whether the problem is factual, structural, stylistic, or personal. They may make a large revision that misses the actual concern. A precise review takes responsibility for naming the gap. That is a form of care for the work and for the person doing it.

## Explain what the requirement protects

Imagine a hypothetical article containing a numerical claim with no source or method. A reviewer could say that the article lacks rigor. That may describe the concern, but it does not explain the next action. A more useful response identifies the particular claim and asks for the evidence, calculation, or qualification needed for a reader to assess it.

The standard becomes easier to understand when its purpose is visible. A source is needed because the claim asks the reader to trust a fact beyond the author's observation. A test is needed because the change affects behavior that could fail in a specific way. A migration plan is needed because the old and new versions may coexist. The requirement protects something concrete rather than merely satisfying the reviewer's preference.

This explanation can reveal that a supposed standard is actually a convention or a personal habit. Conventions can still be useful, especially when consistency helps readers or maintainers. But they should not be presented as universal technical necessities. Distinguish a correctness requirement from a team choice and a stylistic suggestion. People can respond more effectively when the kind of feedback is clear.

A standard should also be appropriate to the work's purpose. A private sketch, an internal proposal, and a public operational guide need different levels of completeness. Requiring publication-level polish from every exploratory note can make early thinking unnecessarily expensive. Accepting exploratory ambiguity in a consequential release document creates a different problem. Name the stage before judging its adequacy.

## Keep the person larger than the current attempt

A weak paragraph is not a complete description of its author. An overlooked edge case is not proof that someone does not care. Reviews become more useful when they remain attached to the artifact and the observed behavior. This does not prohibit discussing repeated patterns. It requires evidence and proportion when doing so.

There is no need to wrap every correction in elaborate praise. Formulaic reassurance can obscure the point and make the conversation harder to trust. Direct language can be considerate: this calculation uses different populations in the numerator and denominator; please make them consistent or explain the difference. The sentence is clear without being contemptuous. It names the problem and leaves room for the author to reason.

Tone alone is not enough. A politely worded demand can still be impossible if it lacks time, access, or a clear priority. If the standard requires work that the team has not allowed for, acknowledge that constraint. Decide what can change: the scope, schedule, resources, or release decision. Respect includes not pretending that an unfunded requirement can be met by enthusiasm.

Reviewers also need to distinguish teaching from taking over. Rewriting the whole artifact may solve the immediate problem while hiding the reasoning from the author. Sometimes direct editing is appropriate, particularly under a shared deadline. When learning matters, explain a representative change and invite the author to apply the principle elsewhere. The goal is usable work and growing judgment, not permanent dependence on the reviewer.

## Make a revision possible to complete

A good review identifies the changes necessary for acceptance. It can also include optional improvements, but those should be clearly separate. Otherwise the author cannot tell when the work is ready. A review that keeps introducing new preferences after each revision can make a standard feel like a moving target even when every individual suggestion is reasonable.

For a practical exercise, take a piece of feedback you are about to give. Write four short lines: the observed gap, the requirement it affects, the consequence of leaving it unchanged, and the evidence that would satisfy the review. If one line is difficult to write, inspect whether the feedback is sufficiently grounded. You may have a useful concern that still needs a clearer formulation.

Then read the feedback from the recipient's position. Can they identify the location of the problem? Can they distinguish a required correction from a suggestion? Do they have enough information to attempt the revision? This is not an invitation to predict their feelings. It is a check on whether the message contains an actionable path.

When the revision arrives, acknowledge the actual improvement. If it meets the standard, say so. Do not withhold closure because another refinement is imaginable. Good work can always be extended, but acceptance requires a boundary. If a new issue appears, explain why it matters and whether it blocks the current purpose or belongs in later work.

The note on [mentorship](/notes/on-mentorship/) explores how support can help someone develop their own judgment. Standards contribute to that development when their reasons are visible. A person can then carry the principle into a new situation rather than memorize one reviewer's preferred answer.

Holding a standard with kindness is demanding because it asks the reviewer to be clear, fair, and willing to examine their own assumptions. It does not guarantee an easy conversation. It does make the conversation more likely to remain about the work that needs to be done, with a path that both people can see.
