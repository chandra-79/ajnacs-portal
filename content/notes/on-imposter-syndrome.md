I spent a lot of my early career waiting for someone to notice I didn't know what I was doing.

Not because I didn't know what I was doing — I did, most of the time, well enough. But there was always a gap between how much confidence the room seemed to expect and how certain I actually felt. The assumption that the gap was visible and damning took years to revise.

I’ve learned that feeling like an impostor is a poor way to judge my own competence. I've met genuinely mediocre engineers who were absolutely certain of their own ability, and I've met exceptional ones who carried chronic low-level uncertainty about whether they belonged in the rooms they were regularly invited into.

The feeling is, in that sense, useless as a signal about actual competence.

What I've noticed over time is that it changes shape rather than disappearing. The early version is: *am I technically good enough?* That one fades because experience accumulates and you have evidence to counter it. The version that persists is subtler: *am I the right person for this specific thing?* Every new domain, every new responsibility, every novel kind of problem reactivates it.

Starting a PhD is activating it in a new form. Am I rigorous enough for academic research? Do I have the patience for the kind of sustained uncertainty a dissertation requires? Will the practitioner lens I'm bringing be an asset or a limitation in academic work?

I don't know the answers yet. I'm choosing to read that uncertainty as appropriate rather than disqualifying.

The most useful reframe I've found: imposter syndrome is a fairly reliable indicator that you're doing something new. The absence of it, in my experience, usually means you're operating in territory you've already mapped. That's not a bad place to be, but it's not the only one worth being in.

If you're feeling it: that's probably not evidence you don't belong. It's probably evidence that what you're doing is genuinely new enough to be worth doing.

## Put a name to the uncertainty

Consider two people preparing for the same review. One understands the design and is uncertain how to discuss it with people from another part of the business. The other can explain the design fluently but has never tested the one assumption on which the estimate depends. Both may describe the job as intimidating. The next thing to do is different. One can practise explaining the decision and ask somebody to point out missing context. The other should run the test or make the unknown explicit in the plan.

The distinction is easy to miss when you start from the question of whether you belong in the room. Trying to answer that question can consume the time you might have spent identifying the unknown. It is more useful to say what would help you do the work responsibly. You may need an introduction, access to earlier decisions, time to learn a method, or permission to start with a small version of the problem. Those are requests another person can understand and respond to. They also make it easier to recognise the places where your concern pointed at something real.

## Use evidence at the scale of the work

One small exercise is to write down three things before taking on an unfamiliar task. What evidence suggests you can handle part of it? Which part calls for help? What would you know after the first attempt that you cannot know now? Keep the answers close to the work. A reviewer's question about a calculation can tell you where you need a second look. It cannot tell you what you will be capable of after you have learned the method.

There is a place for saying you are taking on too much. A context with unclear expectations or persistent exclusion can create uncertainty that a better personal habit will not fix. Compare notes with someone who knows the environment if you need another account of what is happening. The feeling deserves attention. What matters is choosing a response that fits its cause. A useful first step can be naming the one decision you can make today and the one kind of support that would make the next step possible.
