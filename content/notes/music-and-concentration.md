When people ask me about productivity systems, I usually want to talk about music.

I've tried most of the frameworks. The ones that work for me all have one thing in common: they assume the hardest part is getting started. They're right. But there's a second hard part nobody talks about — staying in the thing once you're in it, especially when the problem gets genuinely difficult and your brain starts looking for any exit.

That's where music does something no app has managed.

For architecture work — the kind where you're holding six competing constraints in your head and trying to find the shape that satisfies all of them — I need something with no lyrics, a consistent tempo, and enough harmonic movement to feel alive without demanding attention. Late Coltrane is too unpredictable. Bach keyboard works, particularly the Well-Tempered Clavier. There's a mathematical structure to it that somehow matches the shape of systems thinking.

For writing — this, right now — I want something warmer. Piano with some ambient texture underneath. Nils Frahm. Ólafur Arnalds. The kind of music that has space in it. Writing shrinks in a busy sonic environment.

For deep code review or reading dense documentation, I go silent or white noise. Music of any kind starts pattern-matching with the text and creates interference.

The common thread is that the right music doesn't make you feel productive — it makes you forget to check whether you're being productive. That's the actual goal. The moment you're monitoring your own focus, you've already lost it.

I have no idea why this works. Something about external rhythm providing a scaffold so the internal rhythm of thinking doesn't have to generate its own. Maybe. I'm not a neuroscientist and I'm suspicious of people who explain this stuff too confidently.

What I know is: Bach for architecture, Frahm for writing, silence for reading. Works for me. Your mileage will vary — but it's worth paying attention to what's actually playing when your best work happens.

## Listen for what the task is already asking of you

A piece of music can work beautifully for one part of a day and be a nuisance five minutes later. The difference may come down to which details the work asks you to follow. Picture an architect sketching several possible routes for a system failure. A predictable sound can make the room easier to settle into. Now give that person two near-identical configuration lines to compare. The relevant change could be one word, and any sound that draws attention to itself can feel unnecessary. The task has changed even if the desk and the playlist have stayed the same.

There is also something useful about having a familiar piece available. If the interesting part of choosing music is deciding what to play next, it can become a job of its own. A familiar recording makes that choice cheap. You can stop it if you catch yourself waiting for a favourite passage or following a performer when you need to follow the argument. Being familiar is useful only while the sound stays where you want it in the room.

## Try a small comparison

Take a piece of work you already have to do and choose two similar stretches. Try one in the environment you would usually choose and one with a different sort of sound. Keep the task narrow. Reading two sections of a technical proposal has a better chance of telling you something than comparing an easy writing session with a difficult debugging session. At the end of each stretch, put the work down and say which part was easy to hold in mind and where you kept having to start over. A sentence is enough.

The answer might be that the surrounding noise was the problem. It might be that a piece worked until the writing reached a knot and that turning it off helped you think. That is already useful. There is little point in squeezing a general claim about attention out of one afternoon. The practical question is what you can do next time a similar task arrives. Sometimes the right setting is a piece of music you know well. Sometimes it is permission to stop watching the session and finish the paragraph.
