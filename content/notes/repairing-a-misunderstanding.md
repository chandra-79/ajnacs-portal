A conversation can go wrong without either person saying something obviously unreasonable. One person believes a decision has been made. The other believes they have been invited to explore an option. One hears a request for help. The other means a transfer of responsibility. The words may be familiar to both people while the agreement underneath them is different.

The first impulse is often to defend the sentence that started the problem. It was clear. It was in the message. It was not meant that way. These statements may be accurate, but they do not necessarily repair the shared understanding. A sentence can have a reasonable intended meaning and still produce a different reasonable interpretation in a particular context.

Repair begins by becoming interested in that difference. What did each person believe would happen next? What information did each have? Which part of the conversation carried the assumption? The purpose is not to erase responsibility or declare every interpretation equally supported. It is to find the actual disagreement before adding more explanations to it.

## Replace the courtroom with a reconstruction

Consider a hypothetical exchange in which someone writes that a draft looks good. The author treats this as approval to publish. The reviewer meant only that the direction seemed promising and expected a later factual review. When publication creates a problem, repeating the original message cannot settle the entire issue. The phrase carried more than one possible commitment.

A useful reconstruction would separate the events. The reviewer gave encouraging feedback. The author inferred release approval. Neither explicitly named the remaining review. The repair might include correcting the published material and agreeing that future approval messages state what has been checked. This is more practical than spending the entire conversation deciding who should have understood an unstated convention.

Reconstruction should include context without becoming an excuse. A hurried message, a difficult deadline, or an unfamiliar language may explain how the misunderstanding arose. It does not automatically remove the need to correct its consequences. Explanation and accountability can coexist. A person can say that they did not intend the effect and still help repair it.

Keep the reconstruction narrow enough to be useful. An ordinary misunderstanding can become a referendum on someone's character if the conversation expands into always and never. Specific events are easier to inspect. What was said, what was understood, what happened, and what needs to change are usually enough to begin. If there is a repeated pattern, it can be discussed with actual examples rather than a sweeping label.

## Make room for the effect without inventing an intention

Being misunderstood can feel unfair, especially when the interpretation seems far from what was meant. It is reasonable to clarify the intention. It is also useful to hear the effect before explaining it away. The other person may be describing a consequence that remains real even after the intended meaning is known.

The reverse matters too. A difficult effect does not give anyone complete knowledge of another person's motives. Saying that a comment left a responsibility unclear is more precise than declaring that the speaker wanted to avoid responsibility. The first claim can be examined against the interaction. The second may be an inference that has not been tested.

A repair conversation can therefore hold two statements together: this is what I understood, and I may not yet understand what you intended. That leaves room for correction without demanding immediate agreement. It also makes it possible to discover that the issue is not a misunderstanding at all. Sometimes the people understand each other and disagree about the work. That is a different conversation, and identifying it is progress.

Not every misunderstanding should be resolved in the same channel. A short clarification may work in a message. A sensitive or repeated problem may need a conversation with enough space for questions. Afterward, a brief written account can preserve the agreement. The goal is not to move everything into meetings; it is to choose a setting that can carry the complexity involved.

## Write the next agreement more clearly

A good repair should change the immediate situation. Who will correct the artifact, contact the affected person, or make the pending decision? What remains unresolved? When will the next check happen? Apologies can matter, but they do not replace these practical details. A warm conversation followed by unchanged ambiguity leaves the same conditions in place.

It can also produce a small improvement in language. Replace looks good with a statement of the review performed. Replace can you take a look with the question the reviewer should answer. Replace we should do this with a named decision owner and an explicit proposal. These phrases need not become rigid templates. Their value lies in making the intended commitment easier to see.

For a reflection exercise, choose a low-stakes misunderstanding from your own recent work. Write two versions of the conversation: what you believed was happening and what the other person may have believed. Mark which parts you know and which are guesses. Then identify one question that would have exposed the difference earlier. If appropriate, ask that question now rather than treating the exercise as private certainty about someone else's mind.

Next rewrite the original request or response in a way that makes its scope clear. Include what is being asked, what is not yet decided, and what the recipient should do next. Do not add an essay of defensive detail. The useful revision is often shorter than the explanation needed after the misunderstanding, because it names the commitment directly.

There are limits to this practice. Clearer language cannot solve a deliberate refusal to honor an agreement, and repeated harmful behavior may require a boundary or formal support. The point is not to reinterpret every conflict as an innocent communication problem. It is to avoid making an ordinary ambiguity harder to repair by assuming a motive before examining the meaning.

The note on [giving and receiving feedback](/notes/on-feedback/) considers another conversation in which intention and effect can separate. Repair asks for a related discipline: describe what happened accurately, remain open to missing context, and make the next agreement usable. The aim is a shared understanding that can guide action, not a winning account of the sentence that went wrong.
